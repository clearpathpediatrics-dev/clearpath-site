The basics, in plain language

What the words on your paperwork mean — so the next conversation makes more sense.

What a food allergy is

A food allergy is when the body's immune system reacts to a specific food as though it were harmful. That's different from a food intolerance, which involves digestion rather than the immune system. Both matter, but they're managed differently — and only your provider can say which one your child has.

Words you'll see on your paperwork

  • Allergen — the specific food that triggers your child's reaction.
  • Reaction — the set of symptoms that happen after exposure.
  • Anaphylaxis — a severe, whole-body reaction that is a medical emergency. Your provider will tell you what signs to watch for and what to do.
  • Emergency action plan — a written, provider-signed page that says exactly what to do and when. This is the single most important document you will carry.
  • Cross-contact — when a safe food picks up traces of the allergen from shared surfaces, utensils, or fryers.
  • "May contain" / "processed in a facility with" — voluntary manufacturer labels. Ask your provider how strictly your family should treat them; guidance differs by child.

What usually changes at home

Most families find the first few weeks come down to four habits:

  1. Reading every label, every time. Recipes change without warning, even on products you've bought for years.
  2. Separating preparation. Many families designate utensils, a cutting board, or a shelf to reduce cross-contact.
  3. Telling everyone who feeds your child. Section ⑥ has ready-to-send letters for this.
  4. Keeping the emergency plan and any prescribed medication where you can reach them. Your provider will tell you what to carry and where.
Note: How strict to be — about labels, restaurants, or shared spaces — is a medical decision that depends on your child. Bring those questions to your provider. Section ③ helps you ask them well.

What to write down — and what to bring to the office

This helps you organize and communicate clearly. It does not tell you whether something is serious — that judgment belongs to your child's provider and their emergency action plan.

Read this first

Your provider's emergency action plan is the authority on when to call the office, when to go in, and when to call 911. Keep it visible and follow it. If you don't have one in writing, that's question #1 in Section ③. In an emergency, call 911 — don't stop to document anything.

Details worth capturing every time

When you contact the office, these are the details they'll almost always ask for. Having them ready turns a scattered phone call into a useful one.

Before you pick up the phone

Have these in front of you so the call is short and productive:

  • Your child's full name and date of birth
  • The allergen(s) as your provider worded them
  • Your Section ④ log entry for what just happened
  • The name of the pharmacy you use
  • The one question you most need answered before you hang up
