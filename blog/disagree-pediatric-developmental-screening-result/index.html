What to Do If You Disagree With a Pediatric Developmental Screening Result

September 26, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric developmental screeningdevelopmental checkswell-child visitparent advocacypediatric care navigation

You know your child better than anyone. So when a developmental screening result at a well-child visit doesn't match what you see every day at home, it's completely normal to feel confused, frustrated, or worried. Maybe the screen flagged something that seems fine to you, or maybe it said everything looked typical when your gut says otherwise. Either way, disagreeing with a result doesn't mean you're overreacting or that anyone did anything wrong. It means you have questions worth asking. Here's how to approach that calmly and productively.

A quick reminder: ClearPath Pediatrics helps you understand, organize, and navigate what your licensed providers tell you. We don't interpret screening results or diagnose anything. The steps below are about communication and organization, not clinical conclusions.

First, understand what a screening actually is

A developmental screening is a snapshot, not a final answer. It's usually a short questionnaire or observation done at a single point in time, often based on your responses about what your child does at home. Because it's a quick tool, a screen can sometimes reflect a rough day, a shy moment, a missed question, or a skill your child has but didn't show that day. That's exactly why your firsthand observations matter so much. When you disagree, the most useful thing you can do is bring specific, everyday examples that either support or gently challenge the result.

Before you say anything, jot down a few concrete moments: what your child does, when, and how often. "She points to three body parts when we read at bedtime" is far more useful to a provider than "I think she's fine."

How to raise your disagreement with your provider

The goal is a conversation, not a confrontation. Most pediatric providers welcome parent input because you see behaviors they never will in a short visit. Try opening with curiosity rather than pushback.

  • Ask what the result means and what it doesn't. "Can you help me understand what this score is measuring?" opens the door.
  • Share your specific examples. Bring your written notes so nothing gets lost in a busy appointment.
  • Ask about the range. "Is this a clear flag, a borderline result, or a watch-and-recheck?"
  • Ask about next steps and timing. "Would you recommend a re-screen, and if so, when?"
  • Ask for it in writing. Request a copy of the screening result and any notes for your records.

Can I ask for a re-screen or a second look?

Yes. It is completely reasonable to ask your provider whether a repeat screening at a later date, a different screening tool, or a referral for a more in-depth evaluation makes sense. Providers often expect this and may already plan a recheck. You can also ask what would need to happen for a referral to a specialist, and how that process works with your insurance. Asking these questions doesn't override your provider's clinical judgment; it simply helps you understand your options and make an informed decision together.

What if my provider and I still don't agree?

If you've shared your observations and still feel uneasy, you have options that don't burn any bridges. You can ask for a follow-up visit specifically to revisit development, request a second opinion from another licensed pediatric provider, or ask about community and early-intervention resources you can explore. Keep every result, note, and recommendation organized in one place so any new provider can quickly see the full picture. The point isn't to "win" a disagreement; it's to keep your child's care moving forward with clarity.

Stay organized so the disagreement leads somewhere

A disagreement is only useful if it turns into a clear next step. After the visit, write down what was decided, when the next check is scheduled, and who is responsible for any referral or paperwork. Keep a simple running log of your child's development between visits so you have real examples ready next time. If a referral was mentioned, note the date and follow up if you don't hear back within a week or two. Good records turn a moment of worry into a concrete plan you can actually act on.
