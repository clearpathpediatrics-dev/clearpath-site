What Is Balance Billing in Pediatric Care — and When Is It Not Allowed?

October 5, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

If you've opened a pediatric medical bill and found a charge that looks bigger than what your insurance Explanation of Benefits (EOB) said you'd owe, you may be looking at something called balance billing. It's one of the most confusing parts of paying for your child's care — and understanding it can help you tell the difference between a bill you simply owe and one that may not be allowed. This guide walks you through the basics so you can organize your paperwork and ask the right questions.

What Is Balance Billing?

Balance billing happens when a healthcare provider bills you for the difference between what they charged and what your insurance agreed to pay. Imagine a provider charges $300 for a service. Your insurance considers $200 a fair amount and pays part of it. If the provider then sends you a bill for the remaining $100 — the "balance" insurance didn't cover — that's balance billing.

This most often comes up with out-of-network providers, because in-network providers usually agree by contract to accept the insurer's negotiated rate and not bill you for the leftover. The key thing to know: not all balance billing is allowed. In certain situations, federal and state protections step in to shield families from these surprise charges.

When Is Balance Billing Not Allowed?

A major federal law called the No Surprises Act (effective January 2022) limits balance billing in specific circumstances. In general, you are protected from surprise balance bills when:

  • Emergency care is provided at an out-of-network facility or by an out-of-network provider. You generally can only be charged your in-network cost-sharing amount.
  • You receive care at an in-network facility, but are treated by an out-of-network provider you didn't choose — for example, an anesthesiologist, radiologist, or lab you never selected.
  • Certain air ambulance services are involved.

In these cases, the provider is typically not allowed to send you a balance bill beyond your normal in-network share. Many states also have their own surprise-billing protections that may apply on top of federal rules.

When Might Balance Billing Still Be Allowed?

Protections don't cover every situation. Balance billing may still be permitted when:

  • You knowingly chose an out-of-network provider for non-emergency, scheduled care and signed a consent form agreeing to the out-of-network costs.
  • You have a plan type the No Surprises Act doesn't fully cover. Protections vary by plan, so it's worth confirming how yours applies.

Because the rules depend on your specific plan, your state, and the type of care, the safest move is to keep every EOB and bill together so you can compare them side by side.

How Do I Know If a Bill Is a Surprise Balance Bill?

Start by placing your insurance EOB next to the provider's bill. The EOB lists what the plan paid, what it adjusted, and what it says is your responsibility (your "patient responsibility"). If the provider's bill asks for more than the patient-responsibility amount on your EOB, that gap may be a balance bill. Note whether the provider was in-network, whether the care was an emergency, and whether you chose that provider. These details help you determine whether a protection might apply.

What Can I Do If I Think a Balance Bill Shouldn't Be Allowed?

First, don't rush to pay a charge you're unsure about. Gather your EOB, the itemized bill, and any consent forms you signed. You can call your insurance company's member services line and ask whether the charge is a protected surprise bill under the No Surprises Act. You can also contact the provider's billing office to ask how the charge was calculated. If you believe you received a bill that violates the rules, the federal No Surprises Help Desk and your state insurance department are resources that handle these disputes.
