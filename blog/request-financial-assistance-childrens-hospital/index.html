How to Request Financial Assistance From a Children's Hospital: A Pediatric Billing Guide for Parents

August 28, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric financial assistancechildren's hospital billingpediatric medical billshospital charity carepediatric care navigation

When a pediatric bill lands in your mailbox, the number can feel overwhelming — especially when you're already juggling appointments, follow-ups, and everyday life. The good news: most children's hospitals have formal financial assistance programs, and you have the right to ask about them. This guide walks you through how to find, request, and organize that help so the process feels less intimidating and more like a series of manageable steps.

Remember, this is educational information about the process. The hospital's financial team makes the decisions about what you qualify for, and your licensed providers handle all clinical care.

What Financial Assistance From a Children's Hospital Usually Includes

Hospitals use different names for these programs — "financial assistance," "charity care," "patient financial services," or a "sliding fee scale." Depending on the hospital and your situation, assistance may reduce your balance, spread it into a no-interest payment plan, or in some cases cover a portion of eligible charges. Many nonprofit children's hospitals are required to have a written financial assistance policy (often called an FAP), and they must tell you how to apply.

Start by looking for these on the hospital website or the back of your bill: the words "financial assistance policy," a patient financial services phone number, or an application form you can download.

Step-by-Step: How to Request Financial Assistance

Here's a practical order of operations that keeps things organized:

  • Locate the policy. Search the hospital website for "financial assistance" or call the number on your billing statement and ask, "Can you tell me how to apply for your financial assistance program?"
  • Ask about deadlines. Many programs accept applications for a set window after the date of service. Ask when yours closes so you don't miss it.
  • Request the application in writing. Ask them to email or mail it, plus a list of required documents.
  • Gather your documents. Common requests include proof of income (pay stubs, tax returns), household size, and sometimes proof of insurance or a denial letter.
  • Submit and confirm receipt. Send it through the method they specify, then call to confirm it arrived and ask for a reference or tracking number.
  • Ask what happens to your bill while you wait. Request that collections activity be paused while your application is under review, and get that confirmation in writing if you can.

What Documents Do I Need to Apply for Hospital Financial Assistance?

Every hospital's list is a little different, but most ask for proof of household income (recent pay stubs, a tax return, or a benefits letter), the number of people in your household, and your identification. If your child's claim was denied or you're uninsured, keep any related letters handy too. Before you send anything, call and ask the hospital for their exact checklist — submitting a complete application the first time is the single best way to avoid delays. Make a copy of everything you send for your own records.

Can I Still Apply if I Already Have Insurance?

Yes — having insurance does not automatically disqualify you. Financial assistance programs often help insured families with large deductibles, coinsurance, out-of-pocket balances, or costs left after a denied claim. When you call, say something like, "We have insurance, but the remaining balance is a hardship for our family. Can we apply for financial assistance?" The financial services team can tell you how your existing coverage factors into their review.

Staying Organized Through the Process

Because these applications involve dates, documents, and follow-up calls, a simple system helps enormously. Keep a single folder (paper or digital) with your bill, the application, copies of what you submitted, and a running log of every call — the date, the person's name, and what they told you. If you're denied, ask whether there's an appeal or reconsideration process; many hospitals allow one.
