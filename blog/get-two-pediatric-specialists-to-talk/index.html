How to Get Two Pediatric Specialists to Talk to Each Other: A Parent's Coordination Guide

October 10, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric care coordinationpediatric specialistscare team communicationpediatric navigationmedical records sharing

If your child sees two (or more) specialists, you've probably felt the gap. One doctor orders a test, the other hasn't seen the results, and you're left repeating the same story in every waiting room. Getting two pediatric specialists to actually talk to each other can feel like a full-time job — but there are a few clear, repeatable steps that make it much more likely to happen. None of this requires you to understand the medicine. Your job is to open the door; their job is to walk through it.

Start by naming your "quarterback"

Before you try to connect two specialists, figure out who sits at the center of your child's care. Usually this is your pediatrician or primary care provider — the person who sees the whole picture. Specialists tend to focus on their own area, so having one provider who coordinates the rest is a huge help.

At your next visit, you can say: "I'd like you to be the main point of contact who keeps everyone updated. Can we agree on that?" Once someone is clearly the coordinator, you have a person to route information to — instead of bouncing messages between offices yourself.

Give each specialist the other's exact contact details

Doctors often want to coordinate but don't have what they need to reach each other quickly. You can remove that friction. For each specialist, gather and carry a simple card with:

  • The provider's full name and practice name
  • Their specialty and clinic phone number
  • The fax number or secure portal for records (offices still rely on these)
  • The name of a nurse, medical assistant, or care coordinator at that office

Then, at each appointment, hand it over: "Here's how to reach Dr. [Name], who is also treating my child. Can your office send a note or call them about [specific question]?" Specific is the magic word — a vague "please coordinate" tends to go nowhere.

How do I actually ask one specialist to contact the other?

Make the request concrete and small enough to act on. Instead of asking two busy doctors to "get on the same page," ask for one defined exchange. For example: "Would you be willing to send Dr. [Name] your note from today and your recommendation about next steps? They're deciding on their plan and want to avoid overlap."

Put it in writing through the patient portal when you can, so there's a timestamp and a record. A short portal message such as, "Requesting that today's visit summary be shared with Dr. [Name] at [clinic]. Thank you," gives the office a clear, trackable task.

Can I just sign something so they can share records?

Yes — and this is often the missing piece. Most offices need a signed release of information (ROI) authorization before they'll share records with another provider. Ask the front desk at each office: "Do you have a records release form so these two providers can share information directly?" Fill one out for each direction (Office A to Office B, and Office B to Office A) so the sharing isn't one-sided.

Once releases are on file, future requests move much faster, because the legal permission is already in place. Keep a copy of each signed form in your own records so you can prove consent if anything gets lost.

Ask for a shared document everyone can see

A single, current summary beats a stack of scattered notes. You can ask your coordinating provider: "Can we keep one updated summary — current medications, recent tests, and each specialist's plan — that everyone references?" Many systems can generate this. When both specialists are looking at the same snapshot, the gaps shrink dramatically.

If your child's care is complex, consider requesting a brief care conference — a scheduled call or meeting where the key providers discuss the plan together. You can ask any office: "Is a care coordination call possible between my child's specialists?"

Follow up — kindly and in writing

Even a great request can stall. A few days later, a short portal note works well: "Just checking whether the records to Dr. [Name] went out — thank you for your help." Warm persistence, with a paper trail, gets results without burning bridges.
