How to Keep Track of What Each Pediatric Specialist Is Responsible For

October 26, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When your child sees a cardiologist, a neurologist, a GI doctor, and a developmental pediatrician — plus the primary pediatrician who ties it all together — it's completely understandable to lose track of who is responsible for what. Many parents end up assuming one specialist is handling something, only to discover months later that no one was. The good news is that a simple, repeatable system can keep every role clear. This isn't about medical knowledge; it's about organization.

Below is a practical way to map out each specialist's lane so you always know who to call, who ordered what, and who is watching which part of your child's care.

Start With a Simple "Who Handles What" List

The foundation of keeping track is a single master list — one place where every specialist is written down alongside their role. For each provider, capture a few plain-language facts:

  • Name and specialty (for example, "Dr. Lee — pulmonology").
  • What they focus on for your child, in your own words, based on what they or your pediatrician told you.
  • What they ordered or follow — equipment, labs, follow-up visits, or referrals.
  • How often your child sees them and when the next visit is.
  • Best contact method — portal, phone, or clinic line.

Keep it on paper, a shared note, or a spreadsheet — whatever both parents can actually reach. The format matters far less than keeping it in one place and updating it after each appointment.

How Do I Know Which Specialist Is Responsible for a Specific Issue?

The clearest way to find out is to ask directly at the visit: "Which part of my child's care are you managing, and which parts belong to someone else?" Write the answer down in the specialist's own words. Responsibilities sometimes overlap — two providers may both weigh in on the same medication or symptom — so it helps to also ask, "If I have a question about this between visits, should I call you or someone else?" That single question prevents most of the confusion parents run into. If you're ever unsure who owns a decision, your child's primary pediatrician is usually the right person to help you sort out the lanes.

Track Decisions, Not Just Appointments

A calendar tells you when visits happen, but it won't tell you what was decided. After each appointment, jot down three things while it's fresh:

  • What the specialist said they're responsible for going forward.
  • Anything they said they'd send to another provider (a referral, a report, a result).
  • Anything they're waiting on you or another specialist to do.

That third point is where things commonly slip. When a cardiologist says "I'll coordinate with neurology," note it — then you can gently follow up if it doesn't happen. You're not second-guessing anyone; you're simply making sure the handoffs land.

What Should I Do When Two Specialists Give Overlapping Instructions?

First, don't try to referee the clinical question yourself — that's your providers' job. Instead, document both instructions clearly, note which provider said what and when, and bring the overlap to your primary pediatrician or the specialist most involved in that area. A simple framing works well: "Dr. A and Dr. B both mentioned this. Can you help me understand who's taking the lead so I follow the right plan?" Keeping a written record of who said what makes these conversations faster and keeps you from relying on memory during a stressful moment.

Use a One-Page Team Map You Can Share

Once your list is built, condense it into a single page: each specialist, their lane, and their contact. This becomes incredibly useful in an ER, at a school meeting, or when a new provider joins the team. Keep a printed copy in your bag and a digital copy both parents can open. Update it whenever a specialist is added, discharged, or changes what they manage.
