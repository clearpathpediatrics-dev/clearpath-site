How to Appeal a Denied Pediatric Feeding Therapy Request: A Parent's Step-by-Step Guide

October 4, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

Opening a letter that says your child's feeding therapy has been denied can feel like a gut punch — especially when you've already watched mealtimes become stressful and you know your child needs the support. Take a breath. A denial is not a final answer. It's the start of a process, and that process has clear steps you can follow. This guide will help you understand and organize an appeal so the paperwork supports what your child's licensed providers have already recommended.

Start by Reading the Denial Letter Carefully

Every denial letter contains the information you need to respond well — it's just not always easy to spot. Before you do anything else, find and highlight these pieces:

  • The specific reason for the denial. Common wording includes "not medically necessary," "insufficient documentation," or "service not covered under your plan."
  • The appeal deadline. This is often 30, 60, or 180 days from the date on the letter. Write it on your calendar immediately.
  • How to submit your appeal. Look for an address, fax number, or online portal, plus any required forms.
  • The denial or reference number. You'll need this on every document you send.

The reason matters most, because your appeal should answer that exact reason. An appeal that responds to "insufficient documentation" looks different from one responding to "not covered."

Gather the Paperwork That Supports the Request

A strong appeal is really a strong, organized packet. Your goal is to make it easy for the reviewer to see why your child's care team recommended feeding therapy. Collect copies (never your originals) of:

  • The denial letter itself
  • The original prior authorization request and any codes submitted
  • Notes or evaluations from the provider who ordered the therapy
  • Any feeding or swallowing evaluation your child has had
  • Relevant growth records, referral notes, or specialist reports
  • Your insurance plan's coverage document, if you have it

Reach out to the ordering provider's office and let them know the request was denied. Many offices are willing to write a letter of medical necessity or supply updated clinical notes — this documentation comes from your licensed providers, not from you, and it's often the heart of a successful appeal.

What Should a Parent's Appeal Letter Actually Say?

Your written statement doesn't need to argue clinical points — that's your provider's role. Instead, keep it clear and factual. A helpful structure is: identify your child and the denied service (with the reference number), state that you are formally requesting an appeal, note that you've attached supporting documentation from your child's care team, and ask for a written response by the plan's deadline. Describe how the therapy connects to daily life — for example, that mealtimes are a current struggle — without diagnosing or interpreting symptoms. Sign and date it, and keep a copy of everything you send.

How Long Does a Pediatric Feeding Therapy Appeal Take?

Timelines vary by plan and by whether the appeal is standard or expedited. Standard internal appeals are frequently decided within 30 to 60 days, while urgent or expedited reviews can move much faster — sometimes within 72 hours — when a delay could seriously affect your child's health. Your denial letter and plan documents will list the exact timeframes. If you believe waiting would harm your child, ask the plan directly whether you qualify for an expedited appeal, and ask your provider's office to support that request.

Keep Track and Follow Up

Appeals can stall simply because a fax didn't arrive or a form was missing. Protect your effort by keeping a simple log: the date you sent each document, how you sent it, who you spoke with, and what they said. Confirm your packet was received. If the internal appeal is denied, your letter should explain your next options, which often include an external review by an independent party.
