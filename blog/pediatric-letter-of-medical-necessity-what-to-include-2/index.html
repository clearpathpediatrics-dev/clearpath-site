What a Pediatric Letter of Medical Necessity Should Actually Say

October 30, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

If your child's doctor is requesting a therapy, medication, or piece of equipment, your insurer often asks for a letter of medical necessity (sometimes called an LMN). It's one of the most important documents in the whole prior authorization process — and yet many parents never actually see what's in it until a denial lands in the mailbox.

The good news: you don't have to write this letter. Your child's licensed provider does. But understanding what a strong letter should contain helps you spot gaps, ask good questions, and keep the process moving. Here's what a well-built letter usually includes.

The core pieces a strong letter usually contains

While every insurer has its own rules, most clear, complete letters of medical necessity tend to cover the same building blocks:

  • Who the child is: full name, date of birth, insurance ID number, and the policyholder's information.
  • Who is writing it: the ordering provider's name, credentials, specialty, and contact details — written on official letterhead.
  • The diagnosis: clearly stated conditions, usually with the specific diagnostic (ICD) codes that match the request.
  • What is being requested: the exact item, service, medication, or equipment — ideally with the relevant billing or procedure codes and quantity or frequency.
  • Why it is needed: how the request connects to your child's specific condition and daily functioning.
  • What has already been tried: previous treatments, equipment, or approaches, and what happened with them.
  • What is expected to happen: the clinical goal the provider is aiming for.

When these pieces are present and specific, the reviewer has far less reason to ask for "more information."

Why specificity matters so much

Insurers review these letters against their own coverage policies. A letter that says a child "would benefit from" something is weaker than one that ties the request directly to that child's documented diagnosis, measurable needs, and history. Generic language is one of the most common reasons a letter gets flagged.

The strongest letters read like they could only be about your child — not a template. They describe the real-world impact: what your child can and cannot do, what has changed, and how the requested item or service addresses that specific gap. Dates, measurements, and prior attempts all help a reviewer see the full picture.

What a Letter of Medical Necessity Should Actually Say?

At its heart, a letter of medical necessity should clearly answer four questions: Who is this for? What exactly is being requested? Why does this specific child need it? And what has already been tried or considered? It should state the diagnosis with matching codes, name the exact item or service, explain the clinical reasoning in plain, child-specific terms, and be signed and dated by the ordering provider on letterhead. The goal is to leave the reviewer with no unanswered questions.

Should I Write the Letter Myself as a Parent?

No — the letter of medical necessity must come from your child's licensed provider, because it reflects clinical judgment and carries their professional signature. What you can do is support it: offer the provider your home observations, keep a timeline of what's been tried, and provide dates and details they may not have in the chart. Think of yourself as supplying raw material, while your provider does the clinical writing and signing.

How parents can help without overstepping

You can play a real role in making the letter stronger without giving any medical opinion. Consider organizing and sharing:

  • A simple timeline of symptoms, changes, and prior treatments with dates.
  • Notes on how the need shows up in daily life — at home, at school, during sleep or meals.
  • Any earlier equipment or therapies and why they stopped working.
  • The exact item name, model, or service your provider mentioned.

Handing this to your provider's office saves them time and reduces back-and-forth. Before the letter is submitted

If you're able to see the draft, it's reasonable to gently confirm the basics are there: your child's details are correct, the diagnosis codes match the request, the item or service is named precisely, and the letter is signed and dated. Catching a missing policy ID or a mismatched code before submission can save weeks.

Remember, decisions about diagnosis and treatment always belong to your child's licensed providers. Your job is simply to help the paperwork reflect what they've already determined.
