What to Do When Pediatric Specialists Give Conflicting Advice About Your Child

September 3, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric care coordinationconflicting specialist advicepediatric care teamparent advocacypediatric navigation

Few things rattle a parent like hearing one specialist say one thing and another specialist say the opposite about your child. You're not imagining the stress — when experts disagree, it can feel like the whole plan is falling apart. Take a breath. Conflicting advice is common when more than one specialist is involved, and it usually means the pieces of the picture haven't been put together in one place yet. Your job isn't to referee the medicine. Your job is to organize the conflict clearly enough that your child's licensed providers can resolve it together.

Here's how to move from panic to a plan.

First, Write Down Exactly What Each Specialist Said

Before anything else, capture the two recommendations in plain language while they're fresh. Vague memories fuel confusion; specifics get resolved faster. For each specialist, note:

  • Their name, specialty, and the date they said it
  • The exact recommendation, in their words if you can
  • The reason they gave, if any
  • What they said the alternative or risk was

Often what looks like a contradiction is actually two doctors focused on different priorities — one on the heart, one on the lungs, for example. Seeing both statements side by side sometimes reveals they aren't truly opposite at all. And when they genuinely do conflict, this written summary becomes the single most useful thing you can hand to whoever helps sort it out.

Who Should Break the Tie?

Q: When specialists disagree, who is supposed to resolve it?

A: In most pediatric care, your child's primary care pediatrician acts as the "quarterback" of the team. They see the whole child, not just one system, and they're usually the right person to help weigh conflicting recommendations from specialists. Bring your written summary to them and ask directly: "These two recommendations seem to conflict — can you help me understand how they fit together, and who should make the final call?" If the conflict is between two subspecialists in the same hospital, ask whether the practice has a way for them to consult each other directly. You are allowed to request that the doctors talk to one another rather than passing messages through you.

Ask Clarifying Questions Before You Assume It's a Conflict

A surprising number of "conflicts" dissolve with a few good questions. You don't need any medical knowledge to ask them — you just need to be curious out loud. Helpful phrasings include:

  • "Dr. B recommended X. How does that fit with what you're suggesting?"
  • "If you had to choose between these two approaches, which matters most for my child right now?"
  • "Is this a difference in urgency, or a genuine disagreement about the plan?"
  • "What would you need to know from the other specialist to feel confident?"

Write the answers down the same way you wrote the original advice. Keep every provider's response in one running document so nothing gets lost between appointments.

Can I Ask for a Care Conference?

Q: Is it reasonable to get everyone in the same conversation?

A: Yes. When a decision is significant and the specialists genuinely disagree, you can request a care conference — a scheduled conversation (often by phone or video) where the relevant providers discuss your child's plan together. Ask your pediatrician's office or the hospital care coordinator how to set one up. You can attend, listen, and ask questions. Coming prepared with your written summary of each recommendation helps the meeting stay focused and productive rather than circling.

Keep the Decision — and the Reasons — in Writing

Once a direction is chosen, ask for it in writing: what the plan is, who made the call, and why the other option was set aside. This protects your child if you see a new provider later, and it spares you from re-litigating the same debate in six months when nobody remembers the reasoning. A short note in your records — "On [date], Dr. B and pediatrician agreed on plan X because Y" — is enough.

Remember, you're not expected to decide who's medically right. You're the organizer, the note-taker, and the connector who makes sure the experts actually talk. That's a powerful role, and doing it well genuinely changes how smoothly your child's care goes.
