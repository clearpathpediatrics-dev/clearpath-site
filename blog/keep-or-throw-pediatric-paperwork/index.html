What to Keep and What to Throw Away in Your Child's Pediatric Paperwork

September 13, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric paperworkmedical records organizationchild health recordsmedical document storagepediatric care navigation

If your kitchen counter has a pile of after-visit summaries, insurance letters, lab printouts, and appointment reminders — you are not alone. Pediatric paperwork multiplies fast, especially when your child sees more than one provider. The good news: you don't have to keep all of it, and you don't have to guess. This guide walks you through what's genuinely worth holding onto, what to file temporarily, and what you can safely recycle or shred.

Think of your goal as keeping the paperwork that tells your child's story over time, and letting go of the duplicates and reminders that expire the moment they're used.

What Pediatric Paperwork Should You Keep Long-Term?

Some documents are worth keeping for years — sometimes into adulthood — because they capture information that's hard or impossible to reconstruct later. Consider keeping:

  • Immunization records. These are requested by schools, camps, sports programs, and sometimes future employers. A complete history saves you repeated calls to old offices.
  • Birth and newborn screening records. Details from the newborn period can matter for years.
  • Growth charts and developmental milestone summaries your providers have given you.
  • Specialist consult notes and summaries that describe a diagnosis, plan, or ongoing condition in your provider's words.
  • Hospital discharge summaries and operative or procedure reports.
  • Genetic testing results, imaging reports, and major lab results your care team has flagged as significant.
  • Legal and benefits documents — IEP/504 plans, disability determinations, and prior authorization approval letters.

These are the backbone of your child's health history. Keep them in a way you can actually find them again.

What Can You File Temporarily Then Let Go?

Plenty of paperwork is useful for a season and then becomes clutter. A simple rule: if a document exists mainly to confirm something already resolved, it usually has an expiration date.

  • Appointment reminder cards and texts — recycle once the visit happens.
  • Routine after-visit summaries for well-child visits with no concerns — you may keep the most recent one and toss older duplicates, since the key data lives in the portal.
  • Explanation of Benefits (EOB) statements — keep these until the matching bill is paid and reconciled, then consider holding roughly a year in case of billing questions.
  • Paid bills and receipts — keep until reconciled with your records; hold longer if you use them for FSA/HSA or tax purposes.
  • Prescription information sheets for medications no longer in use.

A dated folder — one per year — makes it easy to purge the oldest year when a new one begins.

What Should You Never Just Toss in the Trash?

Anything with your child's name, date of birth, insurance ID, or medical details deserves shredding rather than plain recycling. That includes EOBs, billing statements, insurance cards you're replacing, and pharmacy labels. Protecting your child's information is part of keeping their records safe.

How Long Should I Keep My Child's Medical Records?

There's no single legal rule for families, but a practical approach many parents use is this: keep permanent-history items (immunizations, diagnoses, surgeries, genetic and major test results, legal/benefits documents) indefinitely, and keep financial paperwork (EOBs, bills, receipts) for about one to three years unless you need them for taxes. When in doubt, err toward keeping anything that describes a diagnosis or a plan, and toward tossing anything that's a duplicate or a reminder. Your providers and insurer retain their own copies too, but your organized file is often faster to access than requesting records.

What's the Easiest Way to Organize What I Keep?

Pick one system and stick with it — paper or digital, not both scattered. A three-part setup works well for most families: a permanent section (the keep-forever documents), a current year folder (active bills, recent summaries, pending referrals), and a to-shred bin you empty monthly. If you scan documents, name files with the date and provider so they sort chronologically. Add a one-page summary at the front listing your child's providers, diagnoses, and medications — it becomes the first thing you grab for any new appointment.
