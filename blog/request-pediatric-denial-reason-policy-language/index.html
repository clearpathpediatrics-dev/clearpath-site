How to Request the Written Pediatric Denial Reason and Policy Language

September 22, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric prior authorizationinsurance denialdenial reasonappealspolicy language

When your child's prior authorization is denied, a quick phone call often leaves you with a vague answer like "not medically necessary" or "doesn't meet criteria." That's not enough to build an appeal on. To respond effectively, you need two specific things in writing: the exact reason for the denial and the policy language the insurer used to make that decision. The good news is that you're entitled to request both, and asking for them is more straightforward than it sounds.

Why the Written Reason and Policy Language Matter

A denial usually points to a specific rule the insurer says wasn't met. Without seeing that rule, you're guessing at what to fix. The written denial reason tells you what the insurer decided, and the policy language tells you which standard they measured your child's situation against. Together, they let your child's licensed providers compare what was requested to what the policy actually requires, so any appeal can speak directly to the criteria in question rather than to a vague summary.

Getting these documents also creates a paper trail. If you ever escalate to an external review, having the insurer's own words on file keeps everyone working from the same facts.

What Exactly Should You Ask For?

When you contact your insurer, be specific. You can request:

  • The written denial letter (also called an Explanation of Benefits or adverse determination notice) if you haven't received one.
  • The specific reason the request was denied, in writing, not a general phrase.
  • The exact policy or medical-necessity criteria used, often called the clinical policy bulletin, coverage policy, or medical policy number.
  • A copy of that policy document itself, or the section that applies to your child's request.
  • The credentials of the reviewer who made the decision, if available.

Write down the request date, the representative's name, and any reference number. Ask them to send the documents by mail, secure portal, or email, and confirm the timeline for delivery.

How Do I Actually Request the Written Denial Reason and Policy Language?

Call the member services number on the back of your child's insurance card and say something like: "I'd like to request, in writing, the specific reason for this denial and a copy of the exact policy language or medical-necessity criteria used to make the decision." Follow up in writing—through the member portal's secure message or a dated letter—so your request is documented. Keep it short and factual: your child's name, member ID, the reference number of the denial, and a clear list of the documents you want. Then note the date the insurer says the materials will arrive, and set a reminder to follow up if they don't.

Can the Insurer Refuse to Give Me the Policy Language?

In most cases, no. Members generally have the right to receive the reason for an adverse determination and the criteria or clinical rationale behind it. If a representative says the policy is proprietary or unavailable, ask for that response in writing and ask how to obtain the section that applies to your specific denial. You can also ask whether your plan is governed by state or federal rules that require this disclosure. Keep your tone calm and persistent—you're gathering documents, not arguing your case yet.

Staying Organized While You Wait

Create one folder—paper or digital—labeled with your child's name and the service that was denied. Keep the denial letter, your written request, the reference numbers, and every policy document in it. When the policy language arrives, highlight the section the insurer cited. Share these materials with your child's licensed providers so they can review whether the request matched the criteria. Remember: your role here is to gather and organize the paperwork, not to interpret the clinical standards yourself—that's a conversation for your child's medical team.
