What a Pediatric Discharge Summary Should Contain: A Parent's Guide to the Paperwork That Comes Home

September 27, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When your child is discharged from the hospital, you leave with a stack of papers, a head full of instructions, and probably very little sleep. Somewhere in that pile is one of the most important documents you'll receive: the pediatric discharge summary. It's the written record of your child's stay and the plan for what happens next. Knowing what it should contain helps you spot gaps before you're home and staring at a phone number you can't reach after hours.

This guide walks through the sections a complete discharge summary usually includes, so you can organize the information and follow up with your child's licensed providers about anything that's missing or unclear. This is about understanding the paperwork you already have — not medical advice.

The Core Sections a Discharge Summary Should Include

Discharge summaries vary between hospitals, but a thorough pediatric one generally covers the same building blocks. As you read yours, look for:

  • Your child's identifying details — name, date of birth, medical record number, and the admission and discharge dates.
  • Reason for the hospital stay — a plain summary of why your child was admitted.
  • What happened during the stay — the hospital course, including any tests, procedures, or treatments that were done.
  • Condition at discharge — how your child was doing when they left.
  • Medications — a current list of what your child should be taking, including any new prescriptions and any home medications that were changed or stopped.
  • Follow-up appointments — who your child needs to see, when, and how to schedule.
  • Home care instructions — activity, feeding, wound care, or equipment guidance provided by the care team.
  • Warning signs and who to call — the symptoms the team wants you to watch for and the contact numbers for questions or urgent concerns.

If any of these are blank, vague, or contradict what a nurse told you verbally, that's worth flagging before or right after you go home.

Why the Medication and Follow-Up Sections Deserve Extra Attention

Two areas cause the most confusion once families are home: medications and follow-up. Hospital stays often add, pause, or change medicines, and the discharge list should reflect the final plan — not what your child was taking on day one. Compare it against what you have at home and ask your provider or pharmacist to clarify anything that doesn't match.

The follow-up section should tell you not just that your child needs an appointment, but with whom, how soon, and how to book it. If it says "follow up with specialist in 2 weeks" without a name or number, that's a common gap. Writing down these details in one place keeps them from getting lost.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between discharge instructions and a discharge summary?

Discharge instructions are the practical, parent-facing directions for caring for your child at home — medications, activity, warning signs, and follow-up steps. The discharge summary is the broader medical record of the entire hospital stay, often written for your child's other providers. You may receive both, and they should agree with each other. If they don't, ask the care team which to follow before you leave.

How do I get a copy of my child's pediatric discharge summary if I didn't receive one?

You have the right to request your child's medical records. Contact the hospital's medical records or health information management department and ask specifically for the discharge summary from that admission. Be ready to provide your child's name, date of birth, dates of the stay, and proof that you're the parent or guardian. Some hospitals also post it to an online patient portal within a few days of discharge.

How to Organize the Discharge Summary Once You're Home

A discharge summary only helps if you can find it when you need it. Keep the original in one folder — physical or digital — with the date and admission clearly labeled. Note the follow-up appointments on your calendar the same day you get home, and keep the "who to call" numbers somewhere you can reach quickly, like your phone's contacts. If your child sees several specialists, this document becomes a useful reference to share so everyone is working from the same information.
