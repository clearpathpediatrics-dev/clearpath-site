How to Get Your Child's Pediatric Medical Records Released for a Second Opinion

October 1, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric medical recordssecond opinionrelease of informationspecialist referralparent guide

Seeking a second opinion for your child is a thoughtful, normal part of being an involved parent — not a sign of distrust. But a second-opinion appointment is only as helpful as the information the new provider has in front of them. The good news: getting your child's medical records released is a standard, well-worn process, and you have a clear right to request it. Here's how to do it calmly and completely.

Start by Figuring Out Which Records You Actually Need

Before you make a single call, get specific. A second-opinion specialist usually doesn't need every page your child has ever generated — they need the records that paint an accurate picture of the issue at hand. Think about gathering:

  • Visit notes from the original specialist or treating provider
  • Lab and test results connected to the concern
  • Imaging (and often the actual images on a disc or secure link, not just the written report)
  • Procedure or operative reports, if any
  • Any referral paperwork and recent medication lists

If you're unsure what's relevant, the office you're seeking the second opinion from can tell you exactly what they'd like to review. Asking them first saves you from requesting too little — or paying to copy a mountain of paper you don't need.

How Do I Actually Request My Child's Medical Records?

Most practices and hospitals handle this through a Release of Information (ROI) or Medical Records department. The process generally looks like this:

  • Ask for the authorization form. Call the current provider's office and request their records release form, or download it from their patient portal.
  • Complete it as the parent or legal guardian. Because the patient is a minor, you'll typically sign on your child's behalf. Have your ID ready.
  • Specify the date range and record types you identified in step one, rather than checking "everything."
  • Say where the records should go. You can have them sent directly to the second-opinion office, to yourself, or both. Having your own copy is almost always worth it.
  • Choose a delivery method — secure portal, encrypted email, fax, mailed paper, or a disc for imaging.

Submit the form the way the office prefers, and ask them to confirm they received it.

How Long Does It Take and Will I Be Charged?

Timelines vary by state and by provider, but many offices respond within a couple of weeks, and some much faster through a patient portal. If your second-opinion appointment is already scheduled, mention that date when you submit the request and ask whether it can be prioritized.

On cost: you're often entitled to an electronic copy at little or no charge, especially through a portal. Paper copies, large files, or imaging discs may carry a reasonable fee. Ask about any cost up front so there are no surprises, and always keep a record of when and how you submitted your request.

Smart Steps to Keep the Process Moving

A few habits make this far smoother:

  • Request records to go to both you and the new provider. That way you can confirm the specialist received them — and fill any gaps yourself if something is missing.
  • Verify arrival a few days before the appointment. Call the second-opinion office and ask, "Do you have everything you need for our visit?"
  • Keep your own organized copy. A dated folder of notes, results, and imaging means you're never starting from zero for the next referral.
  • Bring a short summary of your child's history, current medications, and your main questions, so the conversation stays focused.

Releasing records is paperwork — but it's paperwork that puts the right information into the right hands at the right time. That's what lets a second opinion actually be a second look rather than a fresh start.
