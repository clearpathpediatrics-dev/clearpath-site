How to Build a Pediatric Warning-Signs Page After a Hospital Discharge

August 31, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric dischargewarning signs pagecoming home from hospitalcaregiver organizationpediatric care navigation

Coming home after a hospital stay is a relief — and also a lot to hold in your head. Your discharge papers may include a page (or several) about what to watch for, but that information is often buried in dense paperwork, easy to lose, and hard to hand off to a grandparent or babysitter at 9 p.m. A warning-signs page is your fix: one clear, at-a-glance sheet that organizes what your child's care team already told you into something anyone caring for your child can follow.

To be clear, this page doesn't replace medical advice or tell you what a symptom means. It simply gathers the instructions your licensed providers gave you into one calm, usable place.

Start With What Your Discharge Team Already Told You

Before you write anything, pull out your discharge paperwork and any handouts the hospital sent home. Look specifically for sections labeled "call your doctor if," "return to the ER if," "reasons to seek care," or "follow-up." These are the exact words your care team chose — copy them, don't reinterpret them.

As you read, sort what you find into three buckets:

  • Call 911 / go to the ER now — the most urgent instructions your team listed.
  • Call the doctor's office today — things to report promptly but not emergencies.
  • Mention at the next visit — changes worth tracking but not urgent.

If any instruction is unclear or you're not sure which bucket it belongs in, that's a great question for your discharging team or follow-up provider — not something to guess at.

What Should a Pediatric Warning-Signs Page Include?

A warning-signs page works best when it answers three questions fast: what am I watching for, who do I call, and how do I reach them? Build your page with these sections:

  • Child's basics: name, date of birth, weight (if your team asked you to track it), and current diagnoses in plain words.
  • The three action buckets above, written in your care team's own language.
  • Who to call: the pediatrician's office, the specialist who managed the hospital stay, the after-hours line, and the nurse line — with phone numbers.
  • Key equipment or medication reminders your team flagged (for example, "bring the medication list to every visit").
  • Next appointments and the date/time.

Keep it to one page if you can. Use big headings, short lines, and bold the phone numbers so a stressed caregiver can find them in seconds.

How Do I Make the Page Easy for Every Caregiver to Use?

A warning-signs page only helps if the right person can find it in the moment. Print several copies and place one on the refrigerator, one in your child's go-bag, and one wherever a caregiver sits with your child. Save a photo of it on your phone and text it to anyone who helps — a partner, a grandparent, a nurse, a sitter.

Walk each caregiver through it once, out loud. Point to the emergency line, the doctor's office line, and where the next appointment is written. When everyone reads from the same page — literally — you remove the guesswork and the "I wasn't sure who to call" moment.

Keep It Current as Things Change

A warning-signs page is a living document. Medications get adjusted, follow-up visits change the plan, and new instructions arrive. Put a small "last updated" date at the bottom so you always know which version is current, and reprint whenever your provider gives you new guidance.

After each follow-up appointment, take two minutes to ask yourself: did anything on my page change today? If a specialist adds a new instruction, add it in their words and note the date. Toss the old printed copies so no one follows outdated information.
