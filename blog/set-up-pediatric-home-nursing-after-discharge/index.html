How to Set Up Pediatric Home Nursing After a Hospital Discharge: A Parent's Guide

November 3, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

If your child is coming home from the hospital with ongoing care needs, you may have heard the words "home nursing" tossed around during discharge planning. It can feel overwhelming to figure out what that actually means and how to make it happen. The good news: setting up pediatric home nursing follows a fairly predictable path, and understanding the steps ahead of time helps you move through them without scrambling. This guide walks you through how it typically comes together so you can stay organized and keep the focus on your child.

Start With the Orders From Your Child's Care Team

Home nursing almost always begins with a written order from your child's physician — usually the hospital attending or a specialist involved in the discharge. This order describes the type of care your child needs, how often, and for how long. It's the foundation everything else is built on, because agencies and insurers cannot act without it.

While you're still in the hospital, ask the discharge planner or case manager three things: who is writing the home nursing order, what it says, and whether a copy can be included in your discharge paperwork. Having the order in hand (or knowing exactly where it was sent) prevents delays once you're home. Keep it with your other discharge documents in one folder so you're never hunting for it during a phone call.

How Does Insurance Approval for Pediatric Home Nursing Work?

Home nursing is typically covered under your child's health plan or, in some cases, a state Medicaid or waiver program — but it usually requires authorization before services can start. The hospital case manager often submits the initial request, but it's worth confirming rather than assuming. Approval is based on the physician's order and documentation of medical need, and the number of nursing hours approved may differ from what was requested.

To stay ahead of it, ask your case manager: Has authorization been submitted? Which insurer or program is being billed? How many hours per week were approved, and for what time period? Write down the authorization number and the renewal date. If coverage is denied or comes back with fewer hours than expected, that's a decision your providers and insurer handle together — but knowing the specifics early gives you time to ask questions and plan.

Choosing and Connecting With a Home Health Agency

Once orders and authorization are moving, a pediatric home health agency actually staffs and delivers the care. Sometimes the hospital assigns one; other times you can choose from a list of in-network agencies. Not every agency serves children or offers the specialized care some kids need, so it's fair to ask whether an agency has pediatric experience and whether they can staff the hours your child was approved for.

What Should I Have Ready Before Home Nursing Starts?

Before the first nurse arrives, gather your child's discharge summary, the home nursing order, a current medication and equipment list, emergency contact numbers for your child's providers, and any instructions your care team gave you in writing. Having a clean, accessible spot for this information makes the first visit smoother and helps each nurse pick up where the last one left off.

It also helps to prepare a short overview of your child's routine, comfort cues, and daily schedule. Nurses are coming into your home and your child's world — a little context goes a long way. Remember that the nurse carries out the plan your licensed providers set; questions about changes to that plan always go back to your child's care team.

Keeping It Organized Over Time

Home nursing isn't "set it and forget it." Authorizations expire, hours get renewed, and agencies occasionally have staffing gaps. Keep a simple log of authorization dates, agency contacts, and any missed or covered shifts so you can spot problems early and follow up calmly. Coordinating all of this on top of caring for your child is a real job — and you don't have to do it alone.
