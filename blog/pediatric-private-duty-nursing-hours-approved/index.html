How Private Duty Nursing Hours Are Approved for a Pediatric Patient: A Parent's Guide

October 1, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric private duty nursinghome nursing approvalpediatric home health hourspediatric DME and suppliesnursing hours authorization

If your child qualifies for private duty nursing (PDN) at home, you've probably already learned that the hours aren't simply handed to you. They're requested, documented, reviewed, and authorized — usually by your insurer or your state's Medicaid program. The process can feel opaque, especially when you're exhausted and just want help in the door. This guide walks you through how pediatric private duty nursing hours typically get approved, so you know what's happening and where you fit in.

Private duty nursing is skilled, hourly nursing care delivered in the home — different from a short visit from a home health nurse. Because it's intensive, approval is tied closely to your child's documented medical needs and the orders written by their licensed providers. ClearPath Pediatrics helps families understand and organize this paperwork, but the clinical decisions always belong to your child's care team.

Who Actually Decides How Many Hours Are Approved?

Several people and systems play a role, and it helps to know who does what:

  • Your child's physician writes the order (sometimes called a plan of care) describing the skilled nursing your child needs.
  • The home nursing agency assesses your child, documents care needs, and submits the request for hours to the payer.
  • The payer — a private insurer or your state's Medicaid program (often through a managed care plan) — reviews the request against its coverage rules and issues an authorization for a specific number of hours over a set period.

The number of approved hours usually reflects the intensity and frequency of the skilled tasks your child needs, the medical documentation supporting them, and the payer's specific criteria. Approval is almost always time-limited, which is why renewals matter.

What Documentation Supports a Nursing Hours Request?

Approvals rise and fall on paperwork. The stronger and more current the documentation, the smoother the review tends to go. Commonly involved pieces include:

  • A signed physician order / plan of care detailing the skilled nursing tasks.
  • Recent clinical notes from the hospital, specialists, or pediatrician describing your child's condition and care needs.
  • A nursing assessment completed by the agency.
  • Records of equipment and supplies your child uses at home (feeding, respiratory, and similar needs).

You don't generate most of these yourself, but you can make sure they exist, are current, and are shared with the agency. Keeping copies in one place — a binder or a folder on your phone — saves days of back-and-forth when a renewal comes up.

How Long Does Pediatric Private Duty Nursing Approval Take?

Timelines vary widely by payer and by how complete the initial request is. Some authorizations come through in days; others take weeks, especially if the payer asks for additional documentation. If your request stalls, the most common reasons are missing physician signatures, outdated clinical notes, or a gap between what the order describes and what the request asks for. Staying in touch with your nursing agency's intake or authorization coordinator is the single best way to keep things moving.

Why Were Fewer Hours Approved Than Requested?

Payers sometimes approve fewer hours than the agency requested. This usually comes down to the payer's interpretation of the documentation against its own criteria — not a judgment about how hard parenting a medically complex child is. If this happens, you generally have options: ask the agency to clarify or resubmit with additional supporting notes, request a written explanation of the decision, and ask about the appeal or reconsideration process, which every payer is required to have. Deadlines for appeals are often short, so note them as soon as a decision arrives.

How to Stay Organized Through Approvals and Renewals

A few simple habits make the whole cycle less stressful:

  • Write down the authorization period and end date so you can start the renewal early.
  • Keep a running list of your child's providers, orders, and equipment.
  • Save every authorization letter and denial in one place.
  • Keep one point of contact at your nursing agency and one at your health plan.

None of this replaces your care team — it simply helps you advocate clearly and avoid gaps in coverage when an authorization lapses.
