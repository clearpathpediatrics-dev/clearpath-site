How to Track Pediatric Prior Authorization Expiry Dates So Coverage Never Lapses

October 3, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

If your child relies on a medication, therapy, or piece of equipment that needed insurance approval, you've probably already lived through the paperwork once. The part many parents don't realize until it's too late: that approval has an expiration date. When it lapses, pharmacies may refuse a refill, therapy sessions can be billed as uncovered, and you're suddenly scrambling to re-approve something your child never stopped needing.

The good news is that a coverage lapse is almost always preventable with a simple tracking habit. You don't need special software — just a consistent place to write things down and a few reminders set well in advance. Here's how to build a system that keeps you ahead of every renewal.

Start by Finding the Expiry Date on Each Authorization

Every prior authorization has an approved date range. To build your tracker, you first need to know what you're tracking. For each approved item, gather these details:

  • The service, medication, or equipment that was approved
  • The authorization or reference number
  • The start and end dates of the approval
  • The number of visits or units approved (therapies are often limited by visit count, not just by date)
  • The prescribing or ordering provider
  • The insurance plan it was approved under

You can usually find these on the approval letter, in your insurer's member portal, or by calling the member services number on your card. Write them all in one place — a spreadsheet, a notes app, or a page in your care binder.

How Far in Advance Should I Start a Prior Authorization Renewal?

A good rule of thumb is to begin the renewal process 30 to 45 days before the expiry date. Renewals often require a fresh order from your child's provider, updated clinical notes, and processing time on the insurer's side — any one of which can take a week or more. Starting early gives you a buffer if documentation is missing or the request needs follow-up. For complex items like private-duty nursing, specialty medications, or durable medical equipment, leaning toward the 45-day end is wise, since those reviews tend to take longer.

Build a Reminder System That Does the Remembering for You

The single most reliable fix is to move the deadline out of your head and into a system that alerts you. For each authorization, set two reminders: one at 45 days before expiry (to kick off the renewal) and one at 14 days before (a safety net in case the first slipped by). When a reminder fires, your job is simply to start the conversation — contact the prescribing provider's office to request the renewal order and confirm what documentation the insurer will need this time.

What Should I Do If an Authorization Is About to Expire and the Renewal Isn't Approved Yet?

First, don't panic — and don't assume coverage will quietly continue. Call your insurer's member services line to confirm the exact expiry date and ask whether a renewal request is already in process. Then contact the ordering provider's office to confirm they've submitted the updated order and notes. Ask both whether a temporary or expedited review is possible given the time crunch. Keep a written log of who you spoke with, the date, and what they said. If there's a genuine risk of your child running out of a needed medication or service, ask the provider's office how to bridge the gap while the renewal is pending — that's a clinical decision for them to guide, not something to improvise on your own.

Keep the Whole Picture in One Place

Families managing several specialists often juggle multiple authorizations at once, each on its own clock. A single master list — sorted by soonest expiry date — turns a stressful guessing game into a calm, routine check. Review it once a month, update any dates that changed, and you'll rarely be caught off guard.
