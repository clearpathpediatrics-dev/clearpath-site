What to Do If Your Child's Pediatric IEP Is Not Being Followed

September 17, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric IEPIEP not being followedspecial education rightsIEP accountabilityschool health navigation

You went through the meetings, the evaluations, and the paperwork. You have an IEP in hand that spells out exactly what your child is supposed to receive at school. And then you find out it isn't actually happening — the speech sessions were skipped, the accommodations aren't in place, or the aide your child was supposed to have keeps changing. That gap between what's written and what's real is exhausting, and it's more common than you'd think. The good news: an IEP is a legally binding document, and you have concrete steps you can take.

This isn't about blame. Most gaps come from staffing shortages, communication breakdowns, or a teacher who never saw the plan. Your job is to bring the problem into the open, in writing, and keep pressing until the plan is followed as written.

Start by Confirming What the IEP Actually Says

Before you raise a concern, pull out your current signed IEP and read the specific sections that seem to be falling short. Look for the exact service, the frequency (how many minutes, how often), the setting, and who is responsible. Write down what the document promises versus what you're actually seeing at home or hearing from your child.

Being specific changes the conversation. "My child isn't getting enough support" is easy to brush aside. "The IEP says 60 minutes of occupational therapy weekly and the service log shows two sessions in six weeks" is not. If you don't have a clean copy of the current IEP, request one in writing from the school before you go further.

How Do I Prove My Child's IEP Isn't Being Followed?

Documentation is everything. Start keeping a simple, dated log: what service or accommodation was missed, when, and how you learned about it. Save emails, notes home, and anything your child tells you (with the date). You can also request the school's service logs or session records in writing — these show whether related services actually occurred.

Compare those records side by side with the IEP. Patterns matter more than a single missed session; a repeated gap is far harder to dismiss. Keep everything in one folder, physical or digital, so you're not scrambling when it's time to escalate. This organized record is the backbone of every step that follows.

Put Your Concern in Writing to the Right People

A hallway conversation with a teacher rarely fixes an implementation problem. Instead, email your child's case manager and, if needed, the special education director. Keep it factual and calm: name the specific service, state what the IEP requires, describe what's actually happening, and ask for a written response and a plan to correct it.

Requesting a meeting is often the next step. You can ask for an IEP team meeting to address implementation at any time — you don't have to wait for the annual review. Bring your documentation and ask the team how the missed services will be made up and how they'll prevent the gap from recurring.

What If the School Still Doesn't Follow the IEP?

If writing and meeting don't resolve it, you have formal options that don't require a lawyer to begin. You can file a state complaint with your state's department of education, request mediation, or ask for a due process hearing. Each state publishes procedural safeguards that explain these routes; the school is required to give you a copy, and it lists deadlines and contacts.

Many families also connect with their state's Parent Training and Information Center, a free resource that helps parents understand their rights and next steps. You don't have to choose the most formal path first — often a clear written complaint is enough to prompt action.
