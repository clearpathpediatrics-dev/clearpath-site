How to Get Pediatric Medical Records Released for a Second Opinion: A Parent's Step-by-Step Guide

August 25, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric medical recordssecond opinionrecords releasespecialist referralspediatric care navigation

Deciding to seek a second opinion for your child is a thoughtful, loving step — not a criticism of anyone's care. But once you've booked that new appointment, one practical task stands between you and a productive visit: getting your child's medical records into the hands of the new provider. The good news is that this is a routine, well-worn process, and with a little organization you can move it along quickly.

This guide walks you through exactly how to request and release those records so the second-opinion provider has what they need to review your child's history thoroughly.

Start by Knowing What Records You Actually Need

Before you make a single call, get clear on which records matter for this specific second opinion. Sending everything can slow things down; sending too little means a rushed or incomplete review. For most specialist second opinions, families gather:

  • Visit notes from the current specialist and pediatrician related to the concern
  • Imaging (X-rays, MRIs, CT scans) — often the actual images on disc and the radiology reports
  • Lab and test results tied to the diagnosis or symptoms
  • Procedure or surgical reports, if any
  • A current medication list and any relevant treatment plans

When in doubt, ask the new provider's office what they want to see. Many second-opinion offices have a checklist and will tell you precisely what to send.

How Do I Actually Request My Child's Medical Records?

Every provider that holds your child's records has a process for releasing them, usually managed by a medical records or health information management (HIM) department. Here's the typical path:

  • Ask for the release form. Call the current provider or check their patient portal for a "Release of Information" or "Authorization to Disclose" form.
  • Complete it fully. You'll list your child's name and date of birth, the records requested, the date range, who the records should go to (the new provider's name, fax, or address), and your signature as the parent or legal guardian.
  • Specify delivery. Choose whether records go directly to the new office, to you, or both. Sending a copy to yourself keeps you organized for future visits.
  • Submit and confirm. Deliver the form by portal, fax, email, or in person, then confirm they received it and ask for a timeline.

Because your child is a minor, the parent or legal guardian typically signs. If custody arrangements or guardianship apply, the office may ask for documentation.

How Long Does It Take, and Is There a Cost?

Timelines vary. Under federal law, providers generally must respond to records requests within a set window — often around 30 days — but many offices turn routine requests around in a few business days, especially when you send records directly to another provider. If your appointment is soon, say so; many offices can expedite for continuity of care.

Costs also vary. Sending records provider-to-provider for care purposes is frequently free or low-cost, while large copies or imaging discs may carry a modest fee. Ask up front so there are no surprises.

Tips to Keep the Process Moving Smoothly

A few habits make record transfers far less stressful:

  • Request early. Start the moment your second-opinion visit is booked — don't wait until the week before.
  • Keep a log. Note the date you submitted each request, who you spoke with, and the promised turnaround. A simple list saves hours of follow-up.
  • Follow up in writing. A quick portal message or email creates a paper trail and a gentle nudge.
  • Confirm receipt on both ends. Call the new office a few days before the appointment to verify the records arrived and are complete.
  • Hand-carry imaging. If discs are involved, bringing them yourself avoids mail delays.

Keeping your own organized copy of everything means you're never starting from scratch, whether for this second opinion or the next specialist your family sees.
