How to Document Pediatric Equipment Need for an Insurance Request: A Parent's Organizing Guide

October 23, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When your child's care team recommends a piece of equipment — a wheelchair, feeding pump, nebulizer, special seating, or other durable medical equipment (DME) — insurance usually asks you to prove the need in writing before they'll cover it. That paperwork can feel overwhelming when you're already stretched thin. The good news: documenting equipment need is mostly about gathering the right pieces and organizing them clearly, not about proving anything medical yourself. Your licensed providers supply the clinical reasons. Your job is to make sure those reasons are complete, consistent, and easy for a reviewer to follow.

Here's how to approach it calmly and thoroughly.

What documents does an insurance equipment request usually need?

Insurers typically look for a predictable set of items. Gathering them in one place before you submit saves you from back-and-forth delays. A typical request includes:

  • A prescription or order from your child's treating provider for the specific equipment.
  • A letter of medical necessity written by the provider, explaining why the equipment is needed and why alternatives aren't sufficient.
  • Supporting clinical notes — visit notes, evaluations, or therapy assessments that mention the need.
  • The supplier's quote or itemized details, including the equipment model and billing codes (HCPCS codes).
  • Your insurance information and any prior authorization form the plan requires.

You don't write the clinical portions — your provider does. But you can be the person who makes sure every piece exists, matches, and arrives together.

How do I make sure the documentation actually matches?

One of the most common reasons requests stall is small inconsistencies. Insurers read these documents side by side, so consistency matters more than people expect. Before submitting, check that:

  • The equipment named in the prescription matches the equipment in the letter of medical necessity and the supplier's quote.
  • Your child's name, date of birth, and insurance ID are identical on every page.
  • The billing codes on the supplier quote match what the provider ordered.
  • Dates are current — many insurers want a letter or order dated within a recent window.

If something doesn't line up, call the provider's office or supplier and ask for a corrected version before you submit. Fixing it upfront is far faster than restarting after a denial.

What makes a strong letter of medical necessity?

You can't write this letter — it comes from your child's provider — but you can help them include the details insurers look for. When you request the letter, it's reasonable to share what the plan typically wants to see. Strong letters usually describe your child's current function, how the equipment supports daily living or safety, what has been tried, and the expected benefit. If the provider's office asks for input, you can offer plain observations from home: how your child manages now, where the current setup falls short, and what a typical day looks like.

Keep your contribution factual and observational. You're providing context, not clinical conclusions. Your provider translates that into medical terms.

How should I organize everything before submitting?

Treat the request like a packet. Create one folder — paper or digital — labeled with your child's name and the equipment requested. Inside, keep a simple cover sheet listing every document included and the date you gathered it. Number the pages so nothing goes missing in transit. If you submit online or by fax, keep a timestamped confirmation.

Then build a short tracking log: the date you submitted, who you spoke to, reference numbers, and any deadline the insurer gave. If a reviewer calls with a question, you'll have answers in seconds instead of searching through emails.

What if the request is denied?

A denial is not the end — many are overturned on appeal, often because a missing document or mismatched detail was corrected. Read the denial letter carefully for the stated reason, note the appeal deadline, and ask your provider's office whether additional documentation can address the specific concern. Your organized packet makes an appeal dramatically easier to assemble.

Documenting equipment need is detailed work, but it's work you can do a step at a time.
