What a Pediatric Letter of Medical Necessity Should Actually Say

September 22, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

letter of medical necessitypediatric prior authorizationinsurance appealspediatric care navigationmedical necessity letter

If your child's insurer asked for a "letter of medical necessity," you may be wondering what that letter is supposed to contain — and whether the version your provider sent will actually do the job. You can't write this letter yourself (it comes from your child's licensed provider), but understanding what a strong one includes helps you spot gaps before it's submitted. That can save weeks of back-and-forth.

This is an education and organization guide, not medical advice. Your child's clinical team decides what to recommend and why. Your role is to help make sure the letter is complete, clear, and easy for a reviewer to say "yes" to.

The Core Job of a Letter of Medical Necessity

A letter of medical necessity (often shortened to LMN) explains, in the provider's professional words, why a specific item, service, medication, or therapy is needed for your child. Its whole purpose is to connect your child's individual situation to the insurer's coverage rules. A reviewer who has never met your child should be able to read it and understand the request without guessing.

The strongest letters don't just say "my patient needs this." They show the picture: who your child is, what's going on, what's been tried, and why this exact request is the appropriate next step according to the treating provider.

What Belongs Inside a Strong Letter

While every insurer and situation differs, most complete pediatric letters include these building blocks:

  • Child's identifying details: full name, date of birth, and insurance/member ID so the letter matches the file.
  • The provider's credentials: name, specialty, contact information, and their relationship to your child (treating provider, specialist, etc.).
  • The specific request: the exact item, service, medication, or therapy — including product name, model, quantity, frequency, or duration where relevant, plus any billing or procedure codes the insurer expects.
  • Your child's relevant history: the diagnoses and background that make the request appropriate for this particular child.
  • Current status and functional impact: how things stand now and how it affects your child's daily functioning, safety, growth, or development.
  • What's already been tried: prior treatments, equipment, or approaches, and how they worked or didn't — this often addresses "step therapy" or "less costly alternative" rules.
  • The clinical reasoning: why the provider recommends this request specifically, tied to the child's needs.
  • Reference to guidelines or policy: when applicable, mention of clinical guidelines or the insurer's own criteria the request meets.
  • A clear closing statement and the provider's signature and date.

Why Specificity Matters So Much

Denials often happen not because a request is unreasonable, but because the letter is vague. "The patient would benefit from therapy" gives a reviewer nothing to approve. "Twice-weekly sessions for 12 weeks to address specific documented needs, after home strategies were tried" gives them a concrete, reviewable request. The details are what turn a general statement into a decision a reviewer can make.

Q&A: Who Actually Writes the Letter of Medical Necessity?

The letter must come from a licensed provider who is treating your child — usually the physician, specialist, or therapist recommending the item or service. Parents do not write it, because it reflects clinical judgment. What you can do is provide organized information (dates, what you've observed at home, past equipment and outcomes) and review the finished letter for accuracy, correct spelling of your child's name, and completeness before it's sent.

Q&A: What Makes a Letter Get Denied Even When It's Justified?

Common avoidable reasons include missing member IDs, missing or wrong billing codes, no mention of what was tried before, no connection to the insurer's specific criteria, or language that's too general to review. Letters that don't specify quantity, frequency, or duration also stall. Before submission, it helps to compare the letter against the exact requirements listed in your child's plan or denial notice so nothing obvious is missing.

How to Use This as a Checklist

You don't need to critique your provider's clinical wording. Instead, use the list above as a gentle completeness check. If something's missing, you can ask, "Would it help the reviewer if the letter also mentioned what we tried last year?" Framing it as helping the request succeed keeps everyone on the same team.
