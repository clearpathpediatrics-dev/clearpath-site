What to Send Ahead of a Pediatric Second Opinion Appointment: A Parent's Checklist

October 28, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When you've booked a second opinion for your child, the single most useful thing you can do before the appointment is make sure the new specialist actually has your child's information in hand before you walk in. A second opinion only adds value when the doctor can review what's already happened — the tests, the imaging, the notes — and offer a fresh perspective. If they spend the whole visit trying to piece together a timeline, you've lost the appointment you waited for.

This guide walks you through what to gather and send ahead, how to send it, and how to confirm it arrived. It's about organization and preparation, not clinical decisions — those always stay with your child's licensed providers.

What should I send ahead of a pediatric second opinion appointment?

Send a focused, labeled packet that lets the new specialist understand your child quickly. At minimum, that usually includes:

  • A one-page summary of your child's main concern, in your own words: what's happening, when it started, and the specific question you want answered.
  • Relevant specialist notes and visit summaries from the providers already involved — especially the ones directly related to the reason for the second opinion.
  • Test and lab results, including dates, so the new doctor can see trends rather than a single snapshot.
  • Imaging — not just the written report, but the actual images on a disc or through an image-sharing portal, since specialists often want to review scans themselves.
  • A current medication list with names, doses, and who prescribed each one.
  • Referral paperwork and insurance authorization, if the appointment requires it.

Think of it as handing the specialist a clear starting line instead of asking them to find the starting line first.

How far in advance should I send everything?

Aim to have records delivered at least one to two weeks before the appointment. Specialists frequently review files the day before a visit, and offices need time to scan, upload, and route documents to the right provider. Sending a stack of paper the morning of your appointment almost guarantees it won't be read in time.

Call the new office and ask two simple questions: What's the best way to send records, and who should they be addressed to? Some practices prefer a secure portal upload, some use fax, and some have a records email. Getting this right the first time saves days of back-and-forth.

  • Request confirmation of receipt by name — ask the office to confirm the file is in your child's chart, not just that a fax went through.
  • Label everything clearly with your child's full name and date of birth on each document, since loose pages get separated.
  • Keep your own complete copy of exactly what you sent, so you can reference it on visit day if something is missing.
  • Bring a backup — a printed copy or the imaging disc — to the appointment just in case.

If you're requesting records from your current providers to forward, give them specific instructions and dates rather than asking for "everything," which can trigger a slow, oversized file dump that buries what matters.

A simple way to organize the packet

Order your documents the way a busy specialist reads: summary first, then the most recent and most relevant notes, then supporting tests and imaging. A short cover note at the top — "Here is what I'm sending and the question I'm hoping you can help with" — turns a pile of paper into a usable story. At ClearPath Pediatrics, our RN care navigators help families pull exactly these pieces together so nothing critical is missing and nothing irrelevant clutters the file.

Done well, a send-ahead packet means your second opinion appointment begins with real discussion instead of data entry. That's the whole point of going — to get thoughtful input from a provider who had time to prepare.
