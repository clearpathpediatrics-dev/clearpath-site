Who to Call When a Pediatric Feeding Pump or Vent Alarms at Home

October 27, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

An alarm goes off in the middle of the night, and your heart jumps before you're even fully awake. When you have a child relying on a feeding pump or a ventilator at home, those sounds become part of your life — but knowing who to call, and in what order, can turn a panicked moment into a manageable one. This guide won't tell you what an alarm means medically; only your child's care team can do that. What it can do is help you organize your contacts ahead of time so the right help is always one clear step away.

First, Separate a Medical Emergency From an Equipment Problem

Before thinking about phone numbers, it helps to have already asked your child's providers one question in advance: "What signs tell me this is an emergency versus an equipment issue?" Write their answer down and keep it near the equipment. If your child shows any of the warning signs your medical team has told you to treat as an emergency, your first call is 911 — not the equipment company. For families whose children have vents or complex airway needs, many providers also recommend notifying your local emergency services ahead of time so responders know what to expect. Ask your care team whether that applies to you.

Who to Call for the Equipment Itself

Once you and your care team have established that your child is stable and the issue is the device, there's usually a clear line of contacts. Build this list now, before you need it, and tape it to the machine:

  • Your DME (durable medical equipment) provider's 24/7 line. The company that delivered your pump or ventilator is required to have round-the-clock technical support. This number should be on a sticker on the device or in your delivery paperwork.
  • Your home nursing agency, if you have nursing hours and a nurse is scheduled or on call.
  • The prescribing specialist or clinic (pulmonology, GI, or complex care) for clinical questions the DME company can't answer.
  • The manufacturer's support line for device-specific troubleshooting, often printed in the user manual.

Keep these numbers in more than one place: on the device, in your phone, and in a shared document both parents or caregivers can reach.

Q: My child's feeding pump keeps alarming but she seems fine. Who do I call first?

If your child appears well and your care team has already told you how to tell an emergency from a nuisance alarm, the first call for a persistent or confusing equipment alarm is usually your DME provider's 24/7 technical support line. They can walk you through what the specific alarm code or light means on your model and whether the device needs servicing or replacement. If anything about your child's condition concerns you, contact your clinical team or follow the emergency plan your providers gave you instead.

Q: Who do I call if the ventilator alarm won't stop in the middle of the night?

For a ventilator — equipment that supports breathing — follow the emergency action plan your child's pulmonology or respiratory team created with you. That plan should spell out immediate steps, when to use backup equipment, and when to call 911 versus your DME or respiratory therapist. If you don't have a written plan yet, ask your child's respiratory provider to create one with you. Build Your "Alarm Response" Sheet Before You Need It

The calmest families aren't the ones who never hear alarms — they're the ones who decided in advance what to do. Create a single page that lists: the emergency plan from your providers, your 911 note (including any special instructions they gave you), your DME 24/7 line, your nursing agency, your specialist clinic, and the device make and model. Review it with every caregiver, including grandparents and respite carers, so no one freezes when the sound starts.
