How to Write a Parent Input Statement for a Pediatric IEP: A Step-by-Step Parent's Guide

September 28, 2026 · 6 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When your child has an IEP meeting coming up, you may be asked to submit a parent input statement — sometimes called a parent concerns letter or parent report. It can feel intimidating to sum up everything you know about your child on one page. The good news: you are the expert on your child at home, and this statement is simply your chance to put that knowledge in front of the team in an organized way. You don't need special training or perfect wording. You just need to describe what you see clearly and calmly.

This guide walks you through how to structure that statement, what to include, and how to keep it focused so it actually gets read and used.

What Is a Parent Input Statement, and Why Does It Matter?

A parent input statement is a short written summary you give the IEP team that describes your child's strengths, your observations at home, and your concerns or hopes for the school year. It becomes part of the record and helps the team see the whole child — not just the version they see at school.

It matters because school staff spend a few hours a day with your child in one setting. You see mornings, homework battles, meltdowns after a hard day, and small wins nobody else notices. Putting those observations in writing means they don't get lost in a fast-moving meeting, and they stay in the file for future teams to read.

How Do You Actually Write a Parent Input Statement?

Keep it to one page if you can. A clear, focused statement is easier to act on than a long emotional letter. Try this simple structure:

  • Start with strengths. Open with what your child does well — what they love, what motivates them, how they connect. This sets a positive tone and reminds everyone you're all on the same team.
  • Describe what you see at home. Be specific and factual. Instead of "homework is hard," try "reading assignments take about 90 minutes with frequent breaks and reminders."
  • List your top concerns. Pick your three or four biggest priorities. Trying to fix everything at once dilutes your message.
  • Share your hopes for the year. Name a few concrete goals you'd like to see progress on.
  • Note what's working. If a strategy helps at home, mention it — it may translate to school.

Write in plain language and use "I" statements: "I've noticed," "I'm concerned that," "I'd like to understand." You're sharing observations, not diagnosing anything — that's the role of your child's licensed providers and the school team.

What Should You Include From Your Child's Providers?

If your child sees specialists or therapists, you can reference information they've already shared with you — for example, recommendations a provider has put in writing or notes from an evaluation you have on hand. You don't interpret medical results yourself; you simply organize what your providers have documented so the school has the full picture.

A helpful phrasing is: "My child's provider has recommended [what the provider wrote]. I've attached that letter for the team's reference." This keeps clinical decisions with your child's licensed professionals while making sure the school knows what's already been established.

Q&A: How Long Before the Meeting Should I Submit It?

Aim to send your parent input statement a few days to a week before the IEP meeting. Submitting early gives the team time to read it, means your concerns are less likely to be rushed at the end of the meeting, and ensures your words are entered into the record. Email it to your case manager or IEP coordinator and keep a copy of what you sent and when.

Q&A: What Should I Avoid Putting in a Parent Input Statement?

Avoid venting, blame, or ultimatums — even when you're frustrated. A calm, factual tone tends to get better results and keeps the relationship collaborative. Also avoid trying to diagnose your child or prescribe specific medical interventions; stick to describing behavior, patterns, and what you observe. Leave clinical conclusions to your child's licensed providers and the evaluators on the team.

A Simple Template to Start From

If a blank page feels overwhelming, use these prompts:

  • "Things my child does well and enjoys are..."
  • "At home, I've noticed..."
  • "My top concerns this year are..."
  • "Strategies that seem to help are..."
  • "By the end of this year, I hope my child can..."

Fill in each one honestly, trim the extra words, and you have a strong draft. Read it out loud once — if it sounds like you talking about your child, you've got it right.
