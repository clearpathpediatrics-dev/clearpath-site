Pediatric Nurse Navigator vs. Case Manager vs. Social Worker: Who Does What

November 7, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When your child has several providers, a hospital stay, or a complex diagnosis, you suddenly meet a parade of people with similar-sounding titles. A nurse navigator calls on Monday, a case manager leaves a voicemail Wednesday, and a social worker stops by your child's room Friday. It's easy to assume they all do the same thing — and just as easy to call the wrong one when you urgently need something. This guide sorts out who does what, so you can spend less time guessing and more time getting answers.

Keep in mind that titles and job descriptions vary from one hospital or health plan to another. The descriptions below are general patterns, not strict rules. When in doubt, it's always fair to ask any team member, "Which part of this is yours to help with, and who handles the rest?"

What does a pediatric nurse navigator do?

A nurse navigator is typically a registered nurse who helps you move through a confusing system. Think of them as a guide who understands both the clinical language and the logistics. They often help you understand what a specialist referral means, figure out the order appointments should happen in, prepare questions before a visit, and keep the big picture connected when several providers are involved. Because they have clinical training, they can help you make sense of instructions your doctor already gave — without giving new medical advice or changing your plan. Nurse navigators are especially useful when you feel lost about what happens next and in what order.

What does a pediatric case manager do?

A case manager focuses heavily on coordination, authorizations, and resources that keep care moving. In a hospital, a case manager often works on discharge planning — lining up home equipment, home nursing, or follow-up appointments. On the insurance side, a health-plan case manager may help coordinate covered services, prior authorizations, and benefits for a child with ongoing needs. The common thread is logistics and approvals: making sure the pieces your providers ordered actually get arranged, authorized, and connected. If your question involves "Will this be covered?" or "How do we get this set up?", a case manager is often your person.

What does a pediatric social worker do?

A social worker supports your family's practical and emotional well-being around your child's care. That can include connecting you to community resources, transportation or lodging help, financial-assistance programs, counseling referrals, and support when the stress of caregiving feels like too much. Social workers are also skilled at helping families communicate with teams during hard moments. When the challenge is less about the medical steps and more about how your family is coping and what support exists, the social worker is frequently the right first call.

How do I know which one to call?

A simple way to decide: match your question to the person's focus. If you need help understanding and sequencing care, start with a nurse navigator. If you need something arranged, authorized, or covered, reach out to the case manager. If you need support, resources, or help coping, contact the social worker. Many families keep all three names, phone numbers, and roles written on one page in their care binder. When you're not sure, ask whoever picks up: "This is my situation — are you the right person, or can you point me to who is?" Good teams expect that question and won't mind it.

What if my child's team doesn't have all three roles?

Not every clinic or hospital staffs a separate navigator, case manager, and social worker. In smaller settings, one person may wear several hats, or your pediatrician's office may cover coordination informally. If you can't tell who owns a task, ask your primary pediatric provider's office directly: "Who on the team handles coordination and follow-up for my child?" Write down the answer. Knowing the role — not just the name — helps you aim your next request at the right person the first time.
