How to Request a Virtual Pediatric Second Opinion: A Parent's Step-by-Step Guide

October 2, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

virtual pediatric second opinionpediatric telehealthsecond opinionspecialist referralspediatric care navigation

When your child is facing a big diagnosis or a treatment decision, it's natural to want another set of expert eyes — without pulling your family onto a plane. A virtual pediatric second opinion lets a specialist review your child's case remotely, often from a major children's hospital in another city. The good news: the request process is usually more straightforward than parents expect once you know the steps. Here's how to set one up calmly and completely.

Step 1: Decide What You're Hoping to Learn

Before you reach out to anyone, write down the specific questions you want answered. A second opinion works best when it has a clear focus. Are you trying to confirm a diagnosis? Understand whether a recommended procedure is the only option? Clarify conflicting advice you've already received? Jot down two or three plain-language questions and keep them handy — you'll be asked to summarize your goals when you make the request, and having them ready keeps the whole process efficient.

It also helps to note what your child's current providers have already told you. A virtual second opinion is meant to add perspective, not replace the relationship you have with your licensed treating team.

How do I actually request a virtual pediatric second opinion?

Most families start with one of three paths. First, many large children's hospitals have formal "remote" or "virtual" second opinion programs with an online request form — search the hospital's name plus "second opinion program." Second, your child's current specialist or pediatrician can often make a direct referral to a colleague who offers telehealth consultations. Third, you can call the department you're interested in (for example, cardiology or neurology) and ask whether they review outside cases virtually.

When you contact a program, you'll typically fill out an intake form describing your child, your questions, and your insurance situation. A coordinator then tells you which medical records they need and how to submit them. From there, the reviewing physician studies the file and either writes a report or meets with you by video.

Step 2: Gather and Send the Right Records

This is the part that most often slows families down, so starting early pays off. Virtual programs can only be as thorough as the information you send. Commonly requested items include:

  • Recent clinic notes from the specialists involved in the question
  • Imaging such as MRI, CT, or ultrasound — often the actual image files, not just the reports
  • Lab and test results relevant to the condition
  • Pathology slides or reports, if applicable
  • A current medication list and a short history of what's been tried

You'll usually need to sign a records-release authorization so your current providers can send files directly, or so the new program can request them on your behalf. Ask the coordinator exactly what format they want and whether they prefer secure upload, a portal, or disc. Keeping your own organized copies also makes this far less stressful.

Will insurance cover a virtual pediatric second opinion?

It depends on your plan, so this is worth confirming before you commit. Some insurance plans cover second opinions, including virtual ones, especially when a treating physician documents that it's medically appropriate. Others treat remote second opinion programs as a self-pay service with a flat fee. Call the number on the back of your insurance card and ask two questions: whether second opinions are a covered benefit, and whether telehealth consultations from an out-of-state provider are included. Ask the second-opinion program directly about their self-pay price as a backup, so you can compare and plan.

Step 3: Prepare for the Consultation and Follow Up

If your request includes a live video visit, treat it like any specialist appointment. Find a quiet spot with a stable internet connection, have your question list open, and keep a pen ready to take notes — or ask if you may record the session. Afterward, request a written summary of the reviewing physician's findings so you can share it with your child's existing care team.

The final step is bringing the opinion home. A second opinion is most useful when it becomes part of a conversation with your treating providers, who know your child best and will continue guiding day-to-day care.
