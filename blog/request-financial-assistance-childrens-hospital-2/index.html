How to Request Financial Assistance from a Children's Hospital: A Parent's Step-by-Step Guide

October 5, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric financial assistancechildren's hospital billscharity carepediatric medical billshospital financial aid

When your child needs care, a large hospital bill can land on top of an already stressful season — and that's a lot to carry. The good news is that most children's hospitals have a formal financial assistance program (sometimes called charity care, financial aid, or a hardship program). These programs can reduce or, in some cases, fully cover eligible balances for families who qualify. This guide walks you through how to request that help, step by step, so you know exactly where to start.

Keep in mind: this is organizing-and-navigation help, not legal, tax, or clinical advice. Your hospital's financial counselors make the final eligibility decisions.

Step 1: Find the hospital's financial assistance policy

By federal rules, nonprofit hospitals must publish a Financial Assistance Policy (FAP) and make it available to patients. Start by searching the hospital's website for "financial assistance," "charity care," or "billing assistance." You can also call the number on your bill or statement and ask directly: "Can you tell me how to apply for your financial assistance program, and where do I find the application?"

The policy document usually explains who qualifies, which income levels are considered, what balances are eligible, and the deadline to apply. Read the eligibility section closely — many programs use a sliding scale tied to household size and income, so even families who think they earn "too much" sometimes qualify for partial help.

Step 2: Gather the documents you'll likely need

Applications go faster when you have your paperwork ready. While requirements vary by hospital, programs commonly ask for:

  • Proof of income — recent pay stubs, a tax return, or a benefits letter.
  • Household size — the number of people you support.
  • The specific bill(s) — account numbers and dates of service.
  • Insurance information — including any denial letters, if a claim wasn't covered.
  • A completed application form — provided by the hospital.

Make copies of everything you submit, and jot down the date you sent it and who you spoke with. A simple folder (paper or digital) keeps this from slipping through the cracks.

Who do I call to request financial assistance at a children's hospital?

Ask for the hospital's financial counseling department, patient financial services, or a financial advocate. The billing phone number on your statement is a reliable starting point — tell them you'd like to apply for financial assistance and ask them to mail or email you the application. If your child is admitted or recently discharged, a hospital social worker or case manager can often connect you with the right office, too. You can simply say: "I'd like help understanding my options for this bill — who handles financial assistance applications?"

Can I apply for financial assistance after I've already gotten the bill?

In most cases, yes. Many hospital programs accept applications for a window of time after the date of service — often several months — and some will pause collection activity while your application is reviewed. If you're worried about a deadline, ask the financial counselor directly: "What's the deadline to apply, and can you hold the account while my application is pending?" Getting that answer in writing (email is fine) helps protect you while you gather documents.

Step 3: Submit, confirm, and follow up

Once you've submitted the application, ask for a confirmation that it was received and an estimated timeline for a decision. Write down a reminder to check back if you haven't heard anything by that date. If the program approves partial assistance, you can also ask whether an interest-free payment plan is available for the remaining balance. If you're denied, ask what the appeal or reconsideration process looks like — a changed circumstance or a missing document sometimes changes the outcome.
