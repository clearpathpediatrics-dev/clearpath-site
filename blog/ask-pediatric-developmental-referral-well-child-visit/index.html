How to Ask for a Pediatric Developmental Referral at a Well-Child Visit

August 29, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

If something about your child's development has been on your mind, a well-child visit is a natural place to bring it up. But when you're in the room and the clock is ticking, it can be hard to find the words. The good news: you don't need clinical language to ask for a referral. You just need to be clear about what you're noticing and what you'd like to happen next. This guide walks you through how to prepare and how to ask, so you leave the appointment feeling heard.

To be clear, ClearPath Pediatrics doesn't diagnose or decide whether a referral is right for your child — that's a conversation between you and your licensed pediatric provider. What we can help with is the organizing and preparing so that conversation goes smoothly.

Prepare Before the Visit

The single most helpful thing you can do is jot down what you've observed before you walk in. Providers make referral decisions partly based on the specific, everyday details you share — details they can't see in a short appointment.

Try writing down:

  • What you've noticed — in plain words, like "he isn't using many words yet" or "she seems to have trouble with stairs."
  • When you first noticed it and whether it's changed over time.
  • Specific examples from home, daycare, or school.
  • What others have said — a teacher, grandparent, or childcare provider.
  • Your specific question — for example, "Could we talk about a developmental evaluation?"

Bring this list on paper or your phone. You are not expected to interpret what any of it means — just describe what you see. Your provider will do the clinical thinking.

What to Actually Say in the Room

You don't need a perfect script, but having a simple opening line ready can calm the nerves. Something like: "I've been noticing a few things about my child's development, and I'd like to understand whether a developmental evaluation or referral would make sense."

Then share your notes. If your provider agrees a referral is appropriate, you can ask practical follow-up questions so you know what happens next. If they suggest watching and waiting instead, it's completely fair to ask what specific signs would prompt a referral later, and when they'd want to revisit it.

How do I ask for a developmental referral without sounding pushy?

You're not being pushy — you're being a thorough parent, and providers expect these questions. Frame it as a collaboration rather than a demand. Instead of "I want a referral," you might say, "I'd love your thoughts on whether an evaluation could help us understand this better." Lead with your observations, ask openly, and let the provider weigh in. If you still have concerns after the conversation, it's okay to say, "I'd feel more comfortable having this looked at — can we talk about what that would involve?" Being calm, specific, and honest almost always works better than worrying about tone.

What questions should I ask if my provider agrees to a referral?

Once a referral is on the table, a few practical questions help you stay organized:

  • Who will we be referred to, and for what kind of evaluation?
  • Will your office send the referral, or do I need to call to schedule?
  • How long does it usually take to hear back?
  • Is there any paperwork or screening you need from me first?
  • Does this referral need insurance authorization?
  • What should I do if I don't hear anything within a couple of weeks?

Writing the answers down before you leave saves a lot of follow-up confusion later.

After the Visit: Keep Track

Referrals sometimes stall quietly — a fax that never arrives, an authorization that's still pending, a specialist with a long waitlist. Keep a simple record of who you were referred to, the date, and any reference or authorization numbers. If a week or two passes with no word, a short call to your provider's office to confirm the referral was sent is completely reasonable.

Asking for a developmental referral is one of the most caring things a parent can do, even when it feels intimidating.
