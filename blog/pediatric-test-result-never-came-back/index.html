What to Do When a Pediatric Test Result Never Comes Back: A Parent's Follow-Up Guide

September 18, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric care coordinationmissing test resultslab result follow-uppediatric recordsparent navigation

You did everything right. Your child had the bloodwork, the imaging, or the swab. You were told someone would call, or that results would show up in the portal. Days pass. Then a week. And still — nothing. If you're sitting there wondering whether no news is good news or whether something fell through the cracks, take a breath. A missing result usually isn't a crisis. But it does need a nudge, and you're the right person to give it.

This guide walks you through how to organize your follow-up so you're not left guessing. It's about staying on top of the process, not interpreting anything clinically. Your child's licensed providers are always the ones who explain what a result means.

First, Confirm What You Were Actually Told to Expect

Before you chase anything, get clear on the plan you were given. Results move differently depending on the test. Some post to a patient portal automatically. Some are only shared if the provider calls. Others require the ordering doctor to review them before anything is released to you.

Try to pin down four things: what test was done, who ordered it, where it was performed (the clinic, an outside lab, or a hospital), and how and when you were told you'd hear back. If you're not sure, that's fine — it's exactly what your follow-up call will clarify. Jot these details somewhere you'll keep them, because you may need to repeat them to more than one office.

How Long Should You Wait Before Following Up on a Pediatric Test Result?

A reasonable rule of thumb is to follow up if a routine result hasn't appeared within the timeframe you were quoted — and if no timeframe was given, following up after about a week is completely appropriate. You are never being pushy by asking. If the test was ordered because your provider was concerned about something specific, or if your gut says something feels off, don't wait a week — call sooner. Trust that instinct. Following up early never hurts, and it's far easier than untangling a lost result a month later.

Who Do You Call First When a Result Is Missing?

Start with the office that ordered the test, not the lab. The ordering provider is responsible for receiving, reviewing, and communicating results to you, so they're your anchor point. When you call, keep it simple and specific: give your child's name and date of birth, the test, the date it was done, and where it was performed. Then ask directly: "Have the results come back to your office yet, and if so, what are the next steps for us?"

If the ordering office says they never received the result, that's your signal to call the lab or facility that performed the test. Sometimes a result was completed but routed to the wrong provider, an outdated fax number, or a portal you don't have access to. A quick three-way clarification often solves it.

Keep a Simple Paper Trail

Every time you call, write down the date, who you spoke with, and what they told you. This is your most valuable tool. If a result truly slipped through the cracks, a short log of "I called on the 3rd and again on the 8th" turns a frustrating loop into a clear timeline that any staff member can pick up and act on.

A few habits that make this easier:

  • Ask each office to note in your child's chart that you're waiting on this specific result.
  • Request that results also be released to your patient portal so you have your own copy.
  • Ask for a callback name and direct extension when possible, so you're not starting over each time.
  • If you have more than one specialist involved, make sure the result reaches all of them, not just the one who ordered it.

When You Reach a Dead End

Occasionally you'll hit a wall — offices point at each other, calls go unreturned, or you simply can't get a straight answer. That's a normal, frustrating part of a fragmented system, and it's not a reflection of your effort. This is where a steady, organized advocate makes the biggest difference. Keep escalating politely: ask to speak with a nurse, an office manager, or a records department, and repeat your written timeline calmly each time.
