How to Build a Pediatric Warning-Signs Page After a Hospital Stay

November 14, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When your child is discharged from the hospital, you're usually handed a stack of papers and a flood of instructions — often right as you're trying to get everyone home safely. One of the most helpful things you can create in the first day or two is a single warning-signs page: a short, plain-language sheet that spells out what to watch for, what counts as urgent, and exactly who to call.

This isn't about diagnosing anything yourself. It's about organizing what your child's licensed care team has already told you into one place, so no one in your home has to dig through paperwork during a stressful moment.

Start With Your Actual Discharge Instructions

Your warning-signs page should be built entirely from the information your hospital team gave you — not from the internet, and not from memory. Pull out your discharge summary, any printed instruction sheets, and any notes you took.

Look specifically for sections labeled things like "call your doctor if," "return to the ER if," or "reasons to seek care." Those are the backbone of your page. Copy the exact wording your providers used. If something is vague or you're unsure what a phrase means, that's a great question to bring back to your pediatrician or discharge nurse — don't fill the gap with a guess.

Organize It Into Three Simple Zones

Parents often find it easiest to sort the guidance their team provided into three plain buckets. Use whatever headings match what your providers told you:

  • Watch and note at home — things your team said to keep an eye on and track, but that aren't emergencies.
  • Call the care team — signs your providers said should prompt a phone call to the pediatrician, specialist, or clinic, with the phone numbers written right there.
  • Get urgent help now — the specific instructions your team gave for when to call 911 or go to the emergency room.

Write the phone numbers directly next to each zone so no one has to search. Include your pediatrician, the discharging specialist or clinic, the after-hours or nurse line, and the pharmacy.

What Should a Pediatric Warning-Signs Page Actually Include?

A strong warning-signs page typically includes: your child's name and date of birth; the date of discharge and the condition or procedure in your team's own words; the three action zones above drawn straight from your instructions; every relevant phone number; a short list of current medications and timing (as written by your team); and any follow-up appointments already scheduled. Keep it to one page if you can — clarity matters more than completeness. The goal is something a grandparent, babysitter, or partner can read in thirty seconds and know what to do.

Where Should You Keep It So It Actually Gets Used?

Put a printed copy somewhere everyone can see it — many families tape it inside a kitchen cabinet or on the fridge. Then save a photo of it on your phone and text it to any adult who cares for your child, including the other parent and any regular caregiver. If your child uses a day program, school nurse, or home nursing, ask whether they'd like a copy too. The page only works if it's where the worry happens, not buried in a folder.

Keep It Current

A warning-signs page is a living document. Every follow-up visit can change things — a medication might stop, a new symptom to watch for might be added, or a provider might say your child is past a risky window. Make it a habit after each appointment to ask, "Does anything on my home warning-signs page need to change?" Then update the page and re-send the photo to your caregivers so everyone is working from the same version.
