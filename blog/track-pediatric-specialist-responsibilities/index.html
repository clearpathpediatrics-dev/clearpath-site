How to Keep Track of What Each Pediatric Specialist Is Responsible For

September 18, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric specialist coordinationcare team organizationpediatric care navigationspecialist trackingcomplex pediatric care

When your child sees a cardiologist, a neurologist, a gastroenterologist, and a developmental pediatrician — plus your regular pediatrician tying it all together — the questions pile up fast. Who orders the next set of labs? Who adjusts that plan? Who do you call when something changes? It's completely normal to feel like you're the only one holding all the pieces.

The good news: you don't need a medical degree to stay organized. You need a clear map of who is responsible for what. Below is a simple, parent-friendly way to build that map and keep it current.

Start With a One-Page Specialist Roster

The most useful tool you can make is a single page that lists every specialist and, in plain words, what they focus on for your child. For each provider, write down:

  • Name and specialty (for example, "Dr. Lee — pulmonology")
  • What they manage for your child, in your own words
  • Clinic name, phone number, and portal
  • How often your child is seen there
  • What that provider needs from other providers (records, imaging, referrals)

Keep it to one page so it's easy to scan and easy to hand to anyone stepping in — a family member, a school nurse, or a new provider. The goal is not to capture medical detail. It's to capture roles so you always know who owns which piece.

Ask Each Specialist to Define Their Own Lane

The clearest way to know what a specialist is responsible for is to ask them directly. At the next visit, try a simple question: "So I understand your role, what parts of my child's care do you manage — and what should I take to someone else?" Providers appreciate this because it prevents duplicate orders and gaps.

Jot the answer on your roster right away. If two specialists seem to touch the same area, ask, "Between the two of you, who takes the lead on this?" Getting that spoken out loud — and written down — saves you from being the go-between later.

Q&A: Who Is Responsible for Coordinating Between Specialists?

In most cases, your child's primary pediatrician is the central hub who receives reports from specialists and helps keep the overall picture connected. Specialists focus on their own area and communicate back to the pediatrician. That said, communication between offices isn't automatic, so many parents end up doing part of the coordinating themselves. Knowing this ahead of time helps you ask your pediatrician's office how they prefer to receive updates and whether they've seen the latest specialist notes.

Q&A: How Do I Keep Track of Follow-Ups Without Missing Anything?

Create a simple "open items" list separate from your roster. After each appointment, write down any follow-up the provider mentioned: a test to schedule, a referral to expect, a recheck in a certain number of months, or results you're waiting on. Note the date and which office is responsible. Then review the list weekly. When something has been sitting too long, that's your cue to call and ask for a status. This keeps loose ends from slipping through the cracks between offices that don't always talk to each other.

Build a Repeatable Post-Visit Habit

Consistency beats any fancy app. After every appointment, take five minutes to do the same three things:

  • Update the roster if a specialist's role changed.
  • Add new follow-ups to your open-items list with a responsible office and date.
  • Note who is waiting on what — for example, "Cardiology needs the sleep study results before the next visit."

Whether you keep this in a notebook, a shared note both parents can see, or a folder in a portal, the format matters less than doing it every single time.

Bring Your Map Into the Room

Your roster and open-items list are also communication tools. Bring them to visits so you can say, "Neurology is handling this, GI is handling that — can you tell me where your piece fits?" This helps each provider see the full team and reduces the chance of overlap or missed handoffs.
