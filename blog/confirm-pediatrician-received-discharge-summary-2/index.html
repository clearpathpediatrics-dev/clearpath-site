How to Confirm Your Pediatrician Received the Hospital Discharge Summary

November 25, 2026 · 4 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When your child leaves the hospital, the discharge summary is the bridge between the hospital team and your pediatrician. It's the document that tells your regular doctor what happened, what changed, and what needs to be followed up. But that handoff doesn't always happen automatically — summaries get delayed, sent to the wrong fax line, or lost in a busy office inbox. Confirming that your pediatrician actually received it is one of the most useful things you can do in the first few days home.

The good news: you don't need any clinical knowledge to do this. You're simply making sure a piece of paper got where it needed to go. Here's a practical, low-stress way to do exactly that.

Start Before You Even Leave the Hospital

The easiest time to set this up is during discharge itself. Before you walk out the door, ask the nurse or discharge coordinator three simple questions:

  • "Will a copy of the discharge summary be sent to our pediatrician?"
  • "How is it sent — fax, electronic record, or mail — and when?"
  • "Can I have a printed or digital copy for my own records?"

Ask them to confirm they have your pediatrician's correct name, office, and fax number on file. Hospitals often have outdated contact information, and a single wrong digit can send the whole summary into a void. Getting your own copy is key — it means that even if the electronic handoff fails, you can hand the document to your pediatrician yourself.

How Do I Know If My Pediatrician Actually Received the Discharge Summary?

Call your pediatrician's office within two to three business days of coming home. Ask the front desk or medical records staff directly: "My child was discharged from [hospital] on [date]. Can you confirm the discharge summary is in their chart?" Have your child's full name, date of birth, and the discharge date ready so they can look it up quickly.

If they confirm it's there, you're done — ask them to note that you called. If they can't find it, that's valuable information, not a failure. It simply means the handoff didn't complete, and now you know to close the gap before any follow-up appointment.

What to Do If the Summary Didn't Arrive

If your pediatrician's office says they don't have it, you have a few clear paths:

  • Send your own copy. If you got a printed or digital version at discharge, email or drop it off at the office. This is the fastest fix.
  • Call the hospital's medical records department. Ask them to re-send the summary and confirm the fax number or electronic destination they're using. Give them the pediatrician's correct details again.
  • Ask both offices to confirm receipt. A document being "sent" isn't the same as it being "received." Request confirmation on both ends.

Keep a short log as you go — the date you called, who you spoke with, and what they said. It takes thirty seconds and saves enormous confusion if you have to follow up again.

Why Does This Matter So Much?

Your pediatrician uses the discharge summary to understand any new medications, follow-up appointments, test results, or changes in your child's care plan. Without it, your doctor is working from memory and guesswork at the very moment continuity matters most. Confirming receipt means your child's first post-hospital visit can focus on moving forward, not reconstructing what happened.

This is purely about logistics and organization — not about interpreting anything medical. Every clinical question about what the summary means belongs with your licensed pediatrician. Your job here is simply to make sure the document is in their hands so they can do theirs.

A Simple Checklist to Keep Handy

  • Ask at discharge how the summary will be sent and get your own copy.
  • Confirm the hospital has your pediatrician's correct contact info.
  • Call the pediatrician's office within 2–3 business days to confirm it arrived.
  • If it's missing, send your copy and ask the hospital to re-send.
  • Log every call: date, name, and outcome.

Coordinating handoffs like this is exactly the kind of thing that falls on parents during an already overwhelming week.
