How to Get Pediatric Prior Authorization Approved: A Parent's Organizing Guide

August 12, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric prior authorizationpediatric insuranceprior authorization tipspediatric care navigationinsurance paperwork

If you've ever been told your child's medication, procedure, or specialist visit needs a "prior authorization" before insurance will cover it, you're not alone — and you're not doing anything wrong. Prior authorization (sometimes called "pre-auth" or "pre-certification") is simply your insurance company's request for extra paperwork before they agree to pay. The tricky part is that these requests can stall care, involve several people at once, and leave parents feeling stuck in the middle.

The good news: while you can't control the outcome, you can control how organized the process is on your end. And organized requests tend to move faster. This guide walks you through how to keep the paperwork, timelines, and contacts in one place so you're never scrambling for a fax number or a date.

What Is a Pediatric Prior Authorization, and Why Does My Child Need One?

A prior authorization is a step your insurance plan requires before it will cover certain services, medications, or equipment. It is an insurance and billing process — not a medical decision made by ClearPath or by you. Your child's licensed provider determines what care is appropriate, and the insurance company reviews whether that care meets their coverage rules. Plans commonly request prior authorization for things like specialty medications, imaging, certain procedures, and durable medical equipment. If you have questions about why a specific service was recommended, that's always a conversation for your child's provider.

Build a Simple Prior Authorization Tracker

The single most helpful thing you can do is keep every authorization in one running record. For each request, note:

  • What is being requested — the medication, service, or equipment name.
  • Who ordered it — the provider's name and office.
  • Date submitted — when the office says they sent it to insurance.
  • Reference or authorization number — ask for this every time you call.
  • Status — pending, approved, denied, or needs more information.
  • Expiration date — many approvals are only valid for a set window.

A single page or spreadsheet works fine. The point is that when you call your insurer or your provider's office, you can reference exact dates and numbers instead of starting from memory.

Gather the Documentation Before You Need It

Prior authorizations often stall because the insurer asks for supporting information the office hasn't sent yet. You can't write that clinical documentation — that comes from your child's provider — but you can keep copies of everything the office shares with you so you're ready to help connect the dots. Helpful items to keep organized include visit summaries, previous test or imaging results your provider has given you, and any letters of medical necessity the office has written. Keeping these together means that if the insurer says "we never received it," you can quickly point the office to exactly what needs to be resent.

How Long Does a Pediatric Prior Authorization Take?

Timelines vary by insurance plan and by the type of request. Standard reviews often take several business days to a couple of weeks, while urgent requests are typically handled faster — your provider's office can request an expedited review when appropriate. Because timelines differ, the smartest approach is to ask two questions up front: "When did you submit it?" and "When should I follow up if I haven't heard back?" Write those dates in your tracker and set a reminder. Following up politely but consistently is often what keeps a request from getting lost.

What to Do If a Prior Authorization Is Denied

A denial is not always the end of the road — it often means the insurer needs more information or wants the request handled a different way. Keep the denial letter, note the reason listed, and reach out to your child's provider's office to discuss next steps, which may include submitting additional documentation or requesting an appeal. Your role here is organizational: keep the letters, dates, and reference numbers together so whoever handles the appeal has everything at their fingertips.
