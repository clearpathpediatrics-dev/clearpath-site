How to Set Up Home Nursing After a Pediatric Discharge: A Parent's Step-by-Step Guide

September 27, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric home nursingpediatric discharge planninghome health for childrenprivate duty nursingcoming home from hospital

When you learn your child is coming home with nursing support, it's natural to feel both relieved and unsure. Home nursing can be a lifeline, but the setup process moves fast and involves a lot of moving parts — orders, agencies, insurance, and schedules. This guide walks you through how the pieces fit together so you can ask the right questions and feel more prepared when your child arrives home.

Remember: your child's licensed medical team decides whether home nursing is appropriate and what that care includes. Your job as a parent is to help organize and coordinate what they've ordered. That's exactly the kind of navigation this article is built to support.

Start With the Home Nursing Order

Home nursing begins with a written order from your child's discharging provider. This is often called a plan of care or home health order. Before you leave the hospital, ask your discharge team a few grounding questions:

  • Who is writing the order, and when will it be sent?
  • What type of nursing is being arranged — skilled visits (a nurse who comes for specific tasks) or private duty (longer shifts of continuous care)?
  • How many hours or visits per week were requested?
  • Which home health agency, if any, has already been contacted?

Write down the answers, and ask for the contact name of the hospital's case manager or discharge planner. That person is your first point of contact if anything stalls after you get home.

How Do I Choose and Contact a Home Nursing Agency?

Often the hospital case manager gives you a list of agencies that accept your insurance and serve your area. From there, you can call a few to compare. Helpful questions to ask each agency include:

  • Do you accept my child's insurance, and are you in-network?
  • Do you have nurses experienced with children (and with my child's specific equipment or needs)?
  • How soon can staffing begin after discharge?
  • What happens if a scheduled nurse cannot make a shift?
  • Who do I call after hours or on weekends?

Staffing shortages are common, so it's normal for an agency to approve hours it cannot immediately fill. Ask directly how many of your approved hours they realistically expect to cover in the first two weeks. Knowing this early helps you plan backup coverage rather than being surprised at home.

Understanding the Insurance and Authorization Piece

Home nursing usually requires prior authorization from your insurance plan or, for many children, from a Medicaid or waiver program. The agency and hospital case manager typically start this process, but you'll want to track it. Keep a running note with:

  • The date the authorization was requested
  • How many hours or visits were approved
  • The authorization's start and end dates (so you know when to renew)
  • A reference or authorization number

If coverage differs from what was ordered, that gap is worth clarifying right away with your case manager or plan. In Arizona, some children also qualify for programs that layer additional support — your discharge planner can tell you what applies to your family.

What Should I Have Ready at Home Before the First Nurse Arrives?

A little organization makes the first visit smoother for everyone. Before your nurse's first shift, try to have:

  • A copy of the discharge paperwork and plan of care in one folder
  • Your child's medication list and any supplies that came home
  • A clean, accessible space where care tasks will happen
  • A list of your child's providers with phone numbers
  • Your emergency contacts and, if relevant, your child's baseline notes from the hospital team

Keeping this information together helps each nurse — especially if staff changes between shifts — pick up quickly and consistently.

Keeping the Coordination Going

Home nursing is rarely "set it and forget it." Authorizations expire, agencies change staff, and your child's needs evolve. A simple habit of logging shifts, noting any coverage gaps, and calendaring renewal dates can prevent stressful lapses. If juggling agencies, orders, and insurance feels like a second job, you're not imagining it — this is one of the most complex parts of coming home.
