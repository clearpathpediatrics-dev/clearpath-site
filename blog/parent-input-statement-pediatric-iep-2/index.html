How to Write a Parent Input Statement for a Pediatric IEP: A Step-by-Step Guide

November 4, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When your child has an IEP (Individualized Education Program), you are a member of the team — not just an observer. One of the most powerful ways to make your voice count is a parent input statement: a written summary of what you see at home, what matters most to you, and what you hope the plan will accomplish. The good news is you don't need special training or legal language to write one. You just need to organize what you already know about your child.

This guide walks you through what to include, how to keep it focused, and how to make sure it actually becomes part of the record.

What Is a Parent Input Statement (And Why It Matters)?

A parent input statement is a short document you prepare before or during the IEP meeting that shares your perspective on your child's strengths, needs, and progress. Schools are required to consider parent input, but a written statement does something a conversation can't: it becomes part of the permanent record and ensures nothing you wanted to say gets lost in a busy meeting.

Think of it as your organized contribution to the team. It isn't a complaint letter and it isn't a medical report — it's your firsthand knowledge of your child, which no one else at the table has.

What Should a Parent Input Statement Include?

A parent input statement should include your child's strengths, your specific concerns, examples from home, your goals, and any questions you want addressed. You don't need to cover everything — focus on what will help the team understand your child as a whole person.

Here's a practical structure to follow:

  • Your child's strengths. Start positive. What does your child enjoy, do well, or respond to? Teams build better plans when they know what works.
  • Your main concerns. List two or three specific areas — reading, transitions between activities, staying regulated, making friends. Keep each one concrete.
  • Examples from home. Describe what you actually observe: "Homework that takes classmates 20 minutes takes us two hours with frequent breaks." Specifics are far more useful than labels.
  • Your goals for the year. What would progress look like to you? Name something measurable and meaningful to your family.
  • Questions for the team. Anything you want explained or clarified during the meeting.

How Do You Keep It Focused and Readable?

Keep your parent input statement to one page using short paragraphs or bullet points, dated, and written in plain language. A tight, scannable document is far more likely to be read in full and attached to the record than a long narrative.

A few practical tips:

  • Lead with your child's name and the date at the top.
  • Use headings or bullets so a busy reader can find each section quickly.
  • Stick to observations and goals — avoid diagnosing or prescribing. Instead of interpreting what a behavior "means," simply describe what you see.
  • Stay factual and warm. You can be firm about your concerns without the statement becoming adversarial.
  • Proofread for one clear request per concern so the team knows what you're asking them to consider.

How to Submit It So It's On the Record

Writing it is only half the job — you want it documented. Email your statement to your child's case manager a few days before the meeting and ask that it be attached to the IEP and reflected in the meeting notes. Bring printed copies for everyone at the table as a backup. At the meeting, you can ask directly: "Can we note that my written input has been added to the record?" That one sentence ensures your contribution is preserved.

If your child sees specialists or therapists, you may want to align your statement with what those providers have already shared with you — in your own words, describing what you observe at home.
