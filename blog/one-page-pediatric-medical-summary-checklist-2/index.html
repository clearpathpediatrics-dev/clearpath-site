What Belongs on a One-Page Pediatric Medical Summary: A Parent's Checklist

November 5, 2026 · 4 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When your child sees multiple providers, you end up telling the same story over and over — the diagnoses, the medications, the allergies, the specialists. It's exhausting, and in a rushed appointment it's easy to forget something important. A one-page medical summary fixes that. It's a single sheet that lets any provider, school nurse, or covering doctor understand your child quickly, without you reciting everything from memory.

This isn't a medical record and it isn't medical advice — it's an organizational tool that reflects what your child's licensed providers have already told you. Think of it as a cover sheet for your child's health. Here's what to put on it.

The Core Details Every Summary Needs

Start with the basics at the very top, where anyone can find them in seconds:

  • Child's full name, date of birth, and weight (weight matters because many pediatric details are weight-based — just record what your provider last measured).
  • Parent/guardian names and phone numbers, plus a backup emergency contact.
  • Primary care pediatrician with office phone number.
  • Insurance plan name and member ID for quick reference.
  • Preferred hospital or pharmacy, if your family has one.

Keep this block tight. The goal is that a stranger could pick up the page and know who your child is and who to call within ten seconds.

Medical Information to Include

Next comes the heart of the summary. List only what your providers have documented — you're organizing their information, not interpreting it:

  • Active diagnoses or conditions, written in the words your providers use.
  • Current medications: name, dose, and schedule exactly as prescribed. Note anything your child takes as needed.
  • Allergies and reactions — medications, foods, and environmental, with the reaction described.
  • Medical devices or equipment (feeding tube, hearing aids, inhaler spacer, etc.).
  • Specialists: each one's name, specialty, and phone number.
  • Baseline notes — anything that helps a new provider understand what is "normal" for your child, in your providers' terms.
  • Key recent events: last hospitalization or major procedure with the date.

Resist the urge to include every historical detail. A one-pager works because it's short. Supporting records live in your full binder or portal.

Q&A: How Much Detail Should I Put on a One-Page Summary?

Just enough that a new provider can act confidently and then ask you for the rest. Include current medications, active diagnoses, allergies, devices, and your care team's contacts — the things someone needs right now. Leave off old resolved issues, lengthy test results, and anything that would push you onto a second page. If you can't fit it, it probably belongs in your fuller records rather than the summary. The power of this document is speed and clarity, not completeness.

Q&A: Who Should I Give the Medical Summary To?

Keep a copy where you can grab it fast — in your bag, on your phone, and on the fridge. Share it with people who care for your child when you're not the one explaining: a school nurse, a babysitter or grandparent, urgent care staff, or a covering doctor on call. Many parents also bring a fresh printout to specialist appointments so the provider has a snapshot before the visit begins. Always hand it over as background information, and let your licensed providers make the clinical decisions.

Keeping It Current and Easy to Read

A medical summary is only useful if it's accurate, so build a habit around updating it. Put a "last updated" date at the bottom, and review the page after every medication change, new diagnosis, or hospital stay. Use plain formatting — clear headings, short lines, and a readable font size. Avoid cramming; white space helps a stressed reader find what they need.
