How to Get Pediatric Home Equipment and Supplies Delivered Before Discharge Day

October 8, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

Few things make a hospital homecoming smoother than walking through your front door and finding the equipment your child needs already there, set up and ready. When supplies arrive late, discharge day can turn stressful fast. The good news is that delivery timing is something you can help organize ahead of time — and knowing who to ask, and when, makes a real difference.

This guide walks you through the practical steps of coordinating equipment and supply deliveries before discharge day, so you can focus on your child instead of chasing boxes.

Start With Your Discharge Planner or Case Manager

The earliest and most important step is to identify who on your child's hospital team handles equipment orders. This is usually a discharge planner, case manager, or social worker. Ask them directly: "What equipment and supplies are being ordered for home, and which company will be delivering them?"

Write down the name of the supplier — often called a DME (durable medical equipment) company — along with a phone number and the name of your contact. Ask when the order was submitted and when delivery is expected. The more days between the order and discharge, the better your odds that everything arrives on time.

Make a List and Confirm What's Coming

Equipment and supplies usually fall into two buckets: larger items (like a feeding pump, oxygen setup, monitor, or specialized seating) and consumable supplies (like tubing, syringes, filters, or dressing kits). Both need to be in place before you go home.

Ask your team for a written list of what's been ordered. Then keep your own simple checklist that tracks:

  • The item name and quantity
  • Which company is delivering it
  • The expected delivery date
  • Whether it needs setup or a training visit
  • Whether a prior authorization is still pending

Having this on paper helps you spot gaps early — like a pump that arrived without the tubing it needs, or a monitor still waiting on insurance approval.

How Early Should Equipment Be Ordered Before Discharge?

As a general rule, the sooner the better. Many families find that confirming orders several days ahead gives insurance approvals, delivery scheduling, and any required home setup enough time to fall into place. Some items — especially anything requiring prior authorization or a home assessment — can take longer, so it helps to ask about them as soon as discharge is on the horizon. If your team mentions a possible discharge date, that's your cue to ask, "Is everything on track to be delivered before then?"

Confirm Delivery Logistics at Home

Once you know what's coming, call the DME company yourself to confirm the delivery window. A few questions worth asking:

  • Will someone need to be home to receive or sign for the delivery?
  • Does any equipment require setup or a demonstration visit?
  • Will they teach you how to use it, and is that scheduled?
  • Is there a backup contact if something is missing?

If you'll still be at the hospital on delivery day, arrange for a trusted adult to be home, or ask whether delivery can be timed to your return. Clear communication now prevents a locked door and a missed drop-off later.

What If an Item Won't Arrive in Time?

If you learn that something is delayed, tell your discharge planner right away — before you leave the hospital. Ask whether the delay affects the discharge timeline, whether a temporary loaner or starter supply can be provided, and what the interim plan is. Hospitals often have ways to bridge short gaps, but they can only help if they know early. Keep the DME company's number handy so you can follow up directly once you're home, and note the name of anyone you speak with.

Keep One Simple Folder

Bring together delivery confirmations, supplier contacts, and your checklist in one place — a folder or a notes app works fine. When several deliveries, phone numbers, and approval dates are in play, a single organized spot keeps you from juggling it all from memory during an already-full week.
