How to Prepare a Pediatric Handover Document for a New Provider

October 26, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

Starting with a new provider can feel like going back to square one — repeating the same history, dates, and details you've shared a dozen times before. A well-organized handover document changes that. It's a short, parent-prepared summary that gives a new provider the essentials of your child's care in a format they can scan quickly. You're not diagnosing anything or making clinical decisions — you're simply gathering what your child's existing providers have already told you into one clear place.

Think of it as a friendly introduction to your child, written by the person who knows them best: you.

What a Pediatric Handover Document Actually Is

A handover document is a one- to two-page overview that helps a new provider get oriented fast. It isn't a full medical record — it's a map that points to the record. A good one is skimmable, up to date, and focused on facts rather than interpretation.

Most handover documents include a few core sections:

  • Child basics: full name, date of birth, weight (if your providers track it), and preferred name.
  • Current providers: the names, specialties, and contact details of everyone currently involved.
  • Active diagnoses and concerns: listed exactly as your child's providers have worded them.
  • Current medications and supplies: names, doses as prescribed, and the prescribing provider.
  • Allergies and reactions: as documented by your providers.
  • Recent key events: hospital stays, major tests, or procedures with dates.

Keep the language neutral and factual. You're reporting what's already on record, not adding commentary about what any of it means.

How Do I Decide What to Include?

A simple test: include anything a new provider would need to understand your child safely in the first ten minutes. Leave out the rest — they can request the full records later.

Ask yourself three questions as you draft each line: Is this current? Is it something a provider told me, rather than something I'm guessing? Would leaving it out create confusion? If a detail passes those checks, it belongs on the page. If your child has a complex history, resist the urge to include everything — a document that's too long stops being useful. Point to the full records instead with a note like "complete history available on request from [provider]."

Order matters, too. Put the most clinically relevant items — allergies, current medications, active concerns — near the top where they can't be missed.

How Do I Keep the Handover Document Accurate Over Time?

A handover document is only helpful if it reflects reality. The easiest way to keep it current is to update it at the moment something changes — after a medication adjustment, a new referral, or a discharge — rather than trying to reconstruct everything before an appointment.

Date the document clearly at the top so a new provider knows how fresh it is. Keep one "master" version that both parents or caregivers can reach, and update that single copy rather than juggling several versions. Before each new-provider visit, read it through once to confirm nothing has shifted since your last edit.

Building It Step by Step

If you're starting from scratch, work through it in small pieces:

  • Gather your sources. Pull from after-visit summaries, discharge paperwork, your patient portals, and your current medication list.
  • Draft the sections above using the exact wording your providers used.
  • Keep it to one or two pages. Use short bullet points, not paragraphs.
  • Add a contact line for yourself so the new provider knows how to reach you with questions.
  • Save it somewhere both caregivers can access and print a copy to bring to the first visit.

When you hand it over, a simple line helps: "I put together a quick summary of my child's current care — would it help to have this for your file?" Most providers welcome it.
