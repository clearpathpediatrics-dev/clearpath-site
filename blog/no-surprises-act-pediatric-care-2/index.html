What the No Surprises Act Means for Your Child's Pediatric Bills and Emergency Care

September 9, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

No Surprises Act pediatricsurprise medical bills childrenpediatric billing protectionsout-of-network pediatric carepediatric EOB

If you've ever opened a bill after your child's care and felt your stomach drop at a charge you never expected, you're not alone. The No Surprises Act is a federal law designed to protect families from many of those unexpected out-of-network charges. Understanding what it covers — and what it doesn't — can help you feel a lot steadier the next time an unfamiliar bill or an Explanation of Benefits (EOB) lands in your mailbox.

This is an educational overview to help you organize and understand your child's bills. It is not legal or medical advice, and it doesn't replace guidance from your insurer or your child's licensed providers.

What the No Surprises Act actually protects your child from

The No Surprises Act, which took effect in 2022, was created to stop certain kinds of "balance billing" — the practice of an out-of-network provider billing you for the difference between what your insurance paid and what the provider charged. For families, the most important protections tend to show up in three situations:

  • Emergency care. If your child needs emergency treatment, you generally can't be charged out-of-network rates, even if the emergency room or the treating physician isn't in your plan's network.
  • Care at an in-network facility. If you take your child to an in-network hospital or facility, certain providers who treat them there (like an anesthesiologist or radiologist) can't surprise-bill you at out-of-network rates for many services.
  • Air ambulance services. The law also limits surprise bills from air ambulance transport, which can otherwise be extraordinarily expensive.

In these situations, your child's care is typically billed as if the provider were in-network, and you're responsible only for your normal in-network cost-sharing (like your usual copay or deductible).

Does the No Surprises Act cover all of my child's pediatric bills?

No — and this is where many parents get tripped up. The law targets surprise out-of-network bills, not every bill you might disagree with. It does not eliminate your normal cost-sharing, and it does not cover situations where you knowingly and voluntarily chose an out-of-network provider for non-emergency care after being given proper notice.

It also doesn't apply to ground ambulance services in most cases, which is a common source of unexpected charges. And it doesn't change what your plan considers medically necessary or covered. So while it's a powerful shield against certain surprise bills, it isn't a guarantee that every charge will disappear. Reading your EOB carefully — and comparing it to any bill — is still essential.

What is a Good Faith Estimate, and when should I get one?

If your child is uninsured or you plan to pay out of pocket (self-pay), the No Surprises Act gives you the right to a Good Faith Estimate of expected costs before scheduled care. Providers are required to give you this estimate for scheduled services, and you can request one for any care you're considering.

Keep that estimate. If the final bill is significantly higher — generally $400 or more above the estimate for a given provider — you may be able to dispute it through the federal patient-provider dispute resolution process. Holding onto the estimate, the bill, and the dates of service makes that conversation far easier.

How to organize your paperwork if a surprise bill arrives

When an unexpected bill shows up, it's easy to feel pressured to pay right away. A calmer, organized approach usually serves families better. Consider gathering:

  • The bill itself, with the provider name, dates, and itemized charges
  • The matching EOB from your insurance, which shows what was covered and what you owe
  • Any Good Faith Estimate you received beforehand
  • Notes on whether the care was an emergency or delivered at an in-network facility

With these side by side, you can see whether the charge looks like a protected surprise bill — and you'll have everything ready if you need to call your insurer or the provider's billing office.
