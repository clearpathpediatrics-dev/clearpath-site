What to Do If You Disagree With Your Child's Pediatric Developmental Screening Result

November 2, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

You know your child better than anyone. So when a developmental screening result lands differently than you expected — whether it flags something you don't see, or misses something you do — it's natural to feel unsettled. The good news is that a screening is a snapshot, not a final verdict. It's a tool meant to start a conversation, and you're allowed to be part of that conversation. Here's how to move forward calmly and clearly.

First, Understand What a Screening Actually Is

A developmental screening is a brief, standardized check-in — often a questionnaire or a short set of observations — designed to spot areas that might benefit from a closer look. It is not a diagnosis. Results can be influenced by the day your child had, whether they were tired or hungry, how the questions were interpreted, or simply a skill that's emerging unevenly. Knowing this can take some of the pressure off. A result you disagree with doesn't mean a door has closed or a label has been assigned. It means there's a point worth discussing with your child's licensed provider.

How Do I Tell My Pediatrician I Disagree With the Result?

Lead with curiosity rather than confrontation — it keeps the door open and gets you better answers. You might say: "This result surprised me. Can you walk me through what it's measuring and how you interpret it?" Then share specific, concrete examples of what you observe at home. Instead of "she talks fine," try "she uses about 30 words and combines two of them, like 'more juice.'" Ask directly: What does this result mean for next steps? Is a re-screen, a referral, or watchful monitoring on the table? Writing your questions down beforehand helps you stay focused in a short appointment. Your provider wants the fullest picture possible, and your daily observations are genuine data they can't gather in a 15-minute visit.

Gather Your Own Observations Before the Conversation

Before you follow up, spend a few days jotting down what you actually see. Note skills with real examples and rough frequency — how your child plays, communicates, moves, eats, and interacts. Short videos on your phone can be especially helpful, because they show your provider behavior in a relaxed, familiar setting rather than the unfamiliar exam room. If your child sees multiple caregivers — a grandparent, a daycare teacher, a nanny — ask what they notice too. Different settings sometimes draw out different skills, and gathering these perspectives gives everyone a more complete view. Keep these notes organized in one place so you're not scrambling to remember details later.

Can I Ask for a Re-Screen or a Second Opinion?

Yes. If you and your provider agree the result may not reflect your child's typical abilities, you can ask whether repeating the screening at a later date — when your child is rested and comfortable — makes sense. You can also ask for a referral to a specialist, such as a developmental pediatrician or an early intervention evaluation, for a more thorough assessment. Early intervention programs in Arizona can often evaluate at no cost to families, so ask your provider how to connect with them. Seeking additional perspective is a normal, reasonable part of caring for your child — not a sign of distrust. The goal is clarity, and there's no harm in a closer look.

Keep the Focus on Clear Next Steps

Whatever you decide together, leave the conversation with specifics written down: Is there a re-screen scheduled? A referral being sent, and to whom? A timeframe for checking in again? Who should you call if you have questions in the meantime? Ambiguity is what fuels worry, so pin down the plan. If a referral is involved, ask how it gets submitted and how you'll know it went through. Organizing these threads — appointments, referrals, who's responsible for what — is exactly where many parents feel overwhelmed, and it's an area where a little structure goes a long way.

You Don't Have to Sort It Out Alone

Disagreeing with a screening result doesn't make you difficult — it makes you an engaged parent. Trust what you observe, ask questions, and keep the conversation going with your child's licensed providers, who make all clinical decisions about evaluation and care.
