How to Get Two Pediatric Specialists to Talk to Each Other: A Parent's Coordination Guide

September 3, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric care coordinationpediatric specialistscare team communicationpediatric navigationmedical records

When your child sees more than one specialist, it can feel like each doctor is working in their own separate world. One adjusts a plan, the other doesn't hear about it, and suddenly you're the one carrying messages back and forth. It's exhausting — and it can leave you worried that something important is slipping through the cracks. The good news is there are concrete, practical steps you can take to help two specialists actually communicate. This guide walks you through them, in plain language, without any medical decisions on your plate.

Why Specialists Often Don't Communicate Automatically

It's rarely because anyone doesn't care. Specialists are busy, they often work in different health systems, and their electronic records don't always "see" each other. A cardiologist and a gastroenterologist at two separate practices may have no automatic way to view each other's notes. That means the connection frequently depends on someone flagging the need — and that someone is often you. Understanding this helps you stop waiting for a hand-off that may never happen on its own and start setting one up.

Step 1: Get the Names, Roles, and Contact Details in One Place

Before you can connect two people, you need their information side by side. For each specialist, write down:

  • Full name and specialty
  • Practice or hospital name
  • Office phone and fax number
  • The name of their nurse, coordinator, or scheduler (this is often your fastest route)
  • The patient portal they use, if any

Keeping this on a single sheet or in one note on your phone makes every future call and message faster — and lets you tell one office exactly how to reach the other.

How Do I Ask One Specialist to Contact the Other?

Be direct and specific. At the visit or through the portal, say something like: "Dr. Lee is also treating my child for [reason]. Would you be willing to send your notes to her office, or have a quick conversation about the plan? Here is her name, fax, and phone." Specialists respond well to a clear, narrow request. Rather than a vague "can you two talk," name the exact question you want answered — for example, whether one plan affects the timing of the other. Then hand over the contact details so no one has to hunt for them.

Step 2: Use Records Releases to Open the Channel

Specialists usually need your written permission to share records. Ask each office for a release of information form (sometimes called an authorization to share). Fill one out at each practice naming the other specialist, so records can flow both directions. Once that's signed, you can request that recent notes, test results, and current plans be sent from one office to the other. This creates a paper trail both teams can reference, which is often more reliable than a phone call that no one writes down.

Step 3: Give Each Specialist a One-Page Summary

You can make communication dramatically easier by handing each office a short summary you prepare: your child's name and date of birth, the other specialist's name and what they're managing, current medications as your providers have listed them, and the one or two questions you're hoping the two can align on. You're not interpreting anything medically — you're simply organizing what your licensed providers have already told you so both teams start from the same page.

What If They Still Don't Connect?

If messages go unanswered, escalate politely. Ask to speak with the office's nurse or care coordinator, and ask directly: "What's the best way to make sure these two providers exchange information about my child?" You can also loop in your child's primary pediatrician, who often serves as the central hub and can request notes from both specialists. If two practices share the same hospital system, ask whether the doctors can view each other's records internally. Keep a short log of who you contacted and when, so you're never repeating yourself.
