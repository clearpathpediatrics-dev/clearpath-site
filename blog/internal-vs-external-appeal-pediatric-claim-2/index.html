Internal vs. External Appeal for a Pediatric Claim: A Parent's Guide to the Difference

October 3, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric claim appealinternal vs external appealinsurance denialprior authorization appealpediatric insurance navigation

When your child's medical claim or prior authorization is denied, the letter you receive usually mentions two words that sound similar but mean very different things: internal appeal and external appeal. If you're reading that letter at the kitchen table feeling overwhelmed, take a breath. These are simply two steps in a process that exists specifically to give families a second — and sometimes third — look at a decision. Understanding how they differ helps you prepare the right paperwork at the right time.

This article explains the difference so you can organize your next steps. It is not legal or medical advice — always follow the instructions in your own denial letter and plan documents, and lean on your child's licensed providers for any clinical questions.

What Is an Internal Appeal?

An internal appeal is a request you make to your insurance company itself to reconsider a denial. The same organization that said no takes another look, ideally with fresh eyes and any new information you provide. This is almost always the first step after a denial.

During an internal appeal, you typically submit:

  • A copy of the denial letter and the claim or authorization number
  • A letter explaining why you're appealing
  • Supporting documents your child's provider's office can supply, such as medical records or a letter of medical necessity

Plans have deadlines for both when you must file and when they must respond. Those timelines are printed in your denial letter. If the situation is urgent and your child's health could be seriously affected by waiting, many plans allow an expedited internal appeal with a faster turnaround.

What Is an External Appeal?

An external appeal (sometimes called an external review or independent review) happens after the internal appeal, when the plan has upheld its denial. Instead of the insurer deciding again, an independent third party — reviewers with no financial stake in the outcome — examines the case. In many situations their decision is binding, meaning the plan must follow it.

You generally cannot skip straight to an external appeal. Most plans require you to complete the internal process first, unless your case qualifies for an expedited track. The denial letter that upholds your internal appeal should explain how to request external review, including who to contact and the deadline to apply.

Internal vs. External Appeal: What's the Main Difference?

Q: What is the difference between an internal and external appeal for a pediatric claim?

A: An internal appeal asks your insurance company to reconsider its own denial, and it is the first step. An external appeal sends the decision to an independent outside reviewer, and it usually happens only after the internal appeal is denied. Put simply: internal = the insurer looks again; external = a neutral third party looks instead.

Q: Which one do I file first for my child's denied claim?

A: In almost all cases you file the internal appeal first. The external appeal becomes available once the internal appeal is exhausted (or if your case qualifies for an expedited external review due to urgency). Your denial letter will spell out the exact order and deadlines for your specific plan.

How to Stay Organized Through Both Steps

Appeals are won or lost on paperwork and deadlines, so a little organization goes a long way:

  • Date everything. Write down when you received each letter and the deadline to respond.
  • Keep one folder. Collect every denial letter, appeal letter, and supporting document in one place — physical or digital.
  • Log your calls. Note the date, the representative's name, and a reference number for each conversation.
  • Ask your provider's office early. Records and a letter of medical necessity often take time to gather, and they strengthen both internal and external appeals.
  • Confirm receipt. Send appeals in a way you can track, and ask the plan to confirm they received your materials.
