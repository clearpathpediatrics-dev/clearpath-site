How to Appeal a Denied Pediatric DME or Equipment Request: A Parent's Step-by-Step Guide

September 6, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric DME appealdenied equipment requestprior authorization appealpediatric insurance denialdurable medical equipment

Opening a letter that says your child's wheelchair, feeding pump, standing frame, or other durable medical equipment (DME) has been denied can feel like the floor dropped out. Take a breath. A denial is not the end of the road — it's a step in a process that has clear rules, forms, and deadlines. Your job as a parent isn't to argue medicine; it's to organize the paperwork, meet the timelines, and make sure your child's providers have what they need to support the request. Here's how to do exactly that.

Start by Reading the Denial Letter Closely

Every denial letter must tell you why the request was denied and how to appeal it. Before anything else, find these details:

  • The specific reason for the denial (missing documentation, "not medically necessary," out-of-network supplier, coding error, etc.).
  • The appeal deadline — often 30, 60, or 180 days from the date on the letter. Write it on a calendar today.
  • The type of appeal available (internal appeal, peer-to-peer review, or external review) and the exact address, fax, or portal to use.
  • A reference or claim number to include on everything you send.

Keep the original letter. You'll reference it in every call and every submission.

Gather the Documents That Support the Request

Most DME denials come down to paperwork gaps, not a decision that your child doesn't need the equipment. Your role is to help your providers close those gaps. Collect and organize:

  • The prescription or order for the equipment from your child's provider.
  • A letter of medical necessity — this is written by the licensed provider, not by you, and explains why this specific item is needed.
  • Relevant clinic notes, evaluations, or therapy reports that reference the equipment.
  • The DME supplier's documentation and any codes they submitted.
  • Your copy of the plan's coverage policy for that equipment, if available.

Ask the ordering provider's office and the DME supplier to send you copies of everything they submitted so you can spot what may have been missing.

What's the Difference Between an Internal and External Appeal?

An internal appeal asks the same insurance plan to review its own decision again, often with additional documentation from your child's provider. A peer-to-peer review is a version of this where your child's doctor speaks directly with a reviewing physician at the plan. An external appeal is a review by an independent third party outside the insurance company, and it's usually available only after you've completed the internal appeal. Most plans require you to start with the internal appeal first, then move to external review if the denial stands. Your denial letter will spell out the order and the deadlines for each step.

How Do I Actually Submit the Appeal?

Write a short, factual cover letter that includes your child's name, member ID, the claim/reference number, the equipment requested, and a clear sentence stating you are formally appealing the denial. Attach the supporting documents your provider prepared. Submit it the way the letter directs — portal, fax, or certified mail — and keep proof of the date you sent it. Then follow up: call the plan to confirm they received your appeal and ask for the expected decision timeline. If your child's need is urgent, ask whether an expedited (fast) appeal is available. Log every call with the date, the representative's name, and what was said.

Keep Everyone Moving in the Same Direction

Appeals succeed when the provider, the supplier, and the family are aligned. Confirm the provider's office is sending the updated documentation, confirm the supplier is resubmitting correctly, and confirm the plan received it all. A denial can feel personal, but it's usually a fixable process problem. Stay organized, meet every deadline, and lean on your child's providers for the clinical documentation.
