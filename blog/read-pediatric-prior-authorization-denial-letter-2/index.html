How to Read a Pediatric Prior Authorization Denial Letter: A Parent's Guide

October 29, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

Opening an envelope and seeing the word "denied" next to your child's name is stressful. Take a breath. A denial letter is not a dead end, and it is not a judgment about your child's needs. It's a document with a specific structure, and once you know where to look, it becomes much easier to understand what happened and what your options are. This guide walks you through reading the letter calmly and finding the information that matters.

Start by Confirming the Basics

Before anything else, check that the letter is even about the right thing. At the top you'll usually find your child's name, member ID, the date of the letter, and a reference or case number. Write that case number somewhere you'll find it again, because you'll need it for every phone call and follow-up.

Next, identify exactly what was denied. A letter might deny a specific medication, a therapy, a piece of equipment, a procedure, or a referral. Letters sometimes bundle several requests together, so read closely to see whether everything was denied or only part of the request. Note the service dates and the name of the provider who submitted the request.

Find the Reason for the Denial

Every denial letter must state a reason. This is the most important section, usually labeled something like "Reason for Decision" or "Explanation." Common wording you may see includes phrases about "medical necessity," "not a covered benefit," "out of network," "missing information," or "did not meet criteria."

These phrases point you toward very different next steps. A denial for missing paperwork is often resolved quickly once the right document is sent. A denial that references clinical criteria usually means the insurer wants more documentation from your child's provider. You don't need to interpret the clinical side yourself — your job is simply to spot which category the letter falls into so you know who to call.

What Does "Medical Necessity" Mean on a Denial Letter?

When a letter says a service was not "medically necessary," it does not mean your child doesn't need it. It means the insurer's reviewer decided the documentation they received did not match their internal coverage rules for approving it. This is a paperwork-and-criteria decision, not a clinical conversation with your family. The usual path forward is to ask your child's ordering provider what supporting information the insurer is looking for, so that information can be added to an appeal. Keep the focus on gathering and organizing — the clinical reasoning belongs to your licensed provider.

  • The deadline to file an appeal — often counted in days from the letter date. Mark it on your calendar immediately.
  • How to appeal — by phone, mail, fax, or an online portal, and the exact address or number.

Make a photocopy or scan of the entire letter before you write on it. You'll want a clean original in your records.

How Do I Know Who to Call First?

Start with the member services number printed on the letter or on the back of your child's insurance card, and have the case number ready. Ask them to confirm the denial reason in plain language and what specific document or step would address it. Then loop in the provider's office that submitted the request, since appeals usually need updated information from them. Keeping a simple log of who you spoke with, the date, and what they said will save you enormous frustration later.

Turn the Letter Into an Organized Next Step

Once you've identified the service denied, the reason, and the deadline, you have everything you need to move from worry to action. Create a single folder — paper or digital — with the denial letter, your call log, the case number, and any records your provider sends. That organized packet is the backbone of a clean, on-time appeal.
