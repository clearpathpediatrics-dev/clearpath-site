What to Do When a Pediatric Therapy Authorization Runs Out Mid-Treatment: A Parent's Action Plan

October 4, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

Few things rattle a parent like getting a notice that your child's therapy authorization has run out — right in the middle of a treatment plan that's finally working. Maybe it's physical, occupational, speech, or behavioral therapy, and suddenly the clinic says they can't bill your next visit. Take a breath. A lapsed authorization is a paperwork problem, not a dead end. With a clear, organized approach, most families keep their child's care moving. Here's how to work through it step by step.

First, Confirm Exactly What Expired and When

Before you make a single phone call, gather the facts so you're not guessing. Authorizations are tied to specific details, and knowing them turns a stressful situation into a manageable to-do list.

  • The authorization number and the exact expiration date.
  • The number of visits approved versus how many your child has used.
  • The therapy type and CPT/service codes listed on the approval letter.
  • The referring provider whose referral the authorization is linked to.

You'll usually find these on the original approval letter from your insurer or in your member portal. If you can't locate them, call the number on the back of your insurance card and ask a representative to read the authorization details to you. Write everything down, including the date, the representative's name, and a reference number for the call.

Who Is Responsible for Renewing the Authorization?

Q: Is renewing a pediatric therapy authorization the parent's job or the provider's?

In most cases, the therapy clinic or the referring provider's office submits the renewal (often called a "reauthorization" or "continued care request") to the insurer, because it requires clinical documentation — progress notes, updated goals, and a statement of medical necessity that only a licensed provider can write. However, these requests can quietly slip through the cracks when offices are busy, so your role is to confirm and follow up. Call the clinic and ask directly: "Has the reauthorization been submitted, what date did it go in, and is anything missing?" Then call your insurer to confirm they received it. You're the one person watching the whole process from start to finish, which is exactly why staying organized matters so much.

Ask the Right Questions While the Renewal Is Pending

While the reauthorization works its way through review, you want to understand your options so there's no surprise bill. Consider asking:

  • To the clinic: "Can we continue scheduled sessions while the renewal is pending, and what would happen financially if the renewal is delayed or denied?"
  • To the insurer: "Is there a retroactive authorization window that would cover visits during the gap?" and "What is your typical turnaround time for this review?"
  • To both: "What specific documentation is needed to show continued medical necessity?"

Keep every answer in one place — a notebook, a note on your phone, or a single document. When you can quote dates and reference numbers, offices move faster and nothing gets repeated or lost.

What If the Reauthorization Is Denied?

Q: My child's therapy renewal was denied even though therapy is helping. What now?

A denial is not the final word — it's the start of a formal appeals process you have the right to use. Read the denial letter carefully to find the stated reason and the appeal deadline, which can be short. Your child's provider can submit updated progress notes and a letter of medical necessity explaining why continued therapy is appropriate. You can file an internal appeal with the insurer, and if that doesn't resolve it, external review may be available. Throughout, your job is organizing the timeline and documents so the clinical team can make the strongest possible case. The decisions about what therapy your child needs always stay with your licensed providers.

Keep Momentum With Simple Habits

Going forward, note each authorization's expiration date and set a reminder about 30 days ahead so the renewal starts early — long before the last approved visit. Keep copies of every approval and denial letter together. A little bit of tracking now prevents the mid-treatment scramble later.
