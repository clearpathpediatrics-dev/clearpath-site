Who Is Responsible for Your Child's Pediatric Health Care Plan at School Each Day?

September 16, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric health care planschool nurse responsibilities504 planIEP health planschool health navigation

When your child has a medical condition that needs attention during the school day, one question keeps parents up at night: who is actually responsible for making the plan happen every day? You've done the hard work of getting a written plan in place — but a plan is only as good as the people carrying it out. This guide walks you through the roles you're likely to encounter, so you know exactly who to call and what to reasonably expect from each person.

Keep in mind that titles and staffing vary from district to district, and even from one school to another. Think of the roles below as a map, not a rulebook. Your child's licensed providers guide the medical side; the school team handles the day-to-day carrying out of what those providers have written down.

Who is responsible for a child's health care plan at school day to day?

In most schools, the school nurse is the central person responsible for a student's health care plan on a daily basis. The nurse typically develops the plan from the orders and documentation your child's providers supply, trains other staff on what to do, and oversees how it's followed. When a full-time nurse isn't on site every day, a designated, trained staff member — sometimes called a health aide or health assistant — may carry out routine steps under the nurse's direction. The nurse remains the point person who coordinates it all.

So if you have a question about how the plan is being followed, the school nurse is usually your first call. Ask early in the year who fills this role at your specific school, and get their direct contact information.

The other people who share responsibility

Day-to-day health support rarely rests on one person. Depending on your child's plan, several staff members may play a part:

  • Classroom teachers — often the first to notice changes, follow simple accommodations, and know how to reach the nurse quickly.
  • Front office staff — frequently manage where a plan is stored, who gets called, and how substitute staff are informed.
  • Health aides or assistants — carry out routine, trained tasks when the nurse is covering multiple buildings.
  • Coaches, bus drivers, and cafeteria staff — may need a summary of relevant steps for the settings they supervise.
  • The 504 or IEP case manager — coordinates the paperwork and makes sure the health plan connects to the broader educational plan.

Ask the school how information is shared among these people, especially for substitutes, field trips, and after-school activities — the moments when the usual routine changes.

What should I do if I'm not sure who's in charge on a given day?

Start by requesting a simple, written list of who does what and how to reach them. A clear answer looks like: the named nurse, their backup when the nurse is off-site, the office contact, and the case manager. If the school can't tell you who's responsible on a day the nurse isn't there, that's a fair thing to raise politely and get in writing. You're not being difficult — you're closing a gap that protects your child.

It also helps to confirm how the plan travels with your child. Ask where the current plan lives, how staff are alerted to updates, and what happens during transitions like a new grade, a new building, or a change in staff.
