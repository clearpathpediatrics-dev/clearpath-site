What to Write Down Before a Pediatric Well-Child Visit: A Parent's Checklist

November 13, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

Well-child visits move fast. You've waited weeks for the appointment, and then suddenly you're in the room, your child is squirming, and the questions you meant to ask evaporate. The good news: a few minutes of writing things down beforehand can change the whole experience. When you walk in with notes, you lead the conversation instead of scrambling to keep up — and you leave with clear answers instead of a list of regrets in the parking lot.

This is a simple prep list you can start a few days before the visit. You don't need anything fancy — a notes app, an index card, or the back of the appointment reminder all work.

Start With Your Top 3 Questions or Concerns

Providers often have limited time, so the things at the top of your list are the things that get addressed. Before the visit, write down your three biggest questions or concerns in order of importance. These might be about sleep, eating, behavior, speech, mood, screen time, or anything that's been nagging at you.

Be specific and factual. Instead of "he seems off," try "waking up 3–4 times a night for the past two weeks" or "only saying about five words at 18 months." You're not diagnosing anything — you're giving your provider clear, concrete observations they can respond to. If something has changed recently, note roughly when it started.

Track the Everyday Details You'll Be Asked About

Many well-child visits include developmental and routine questions, and it's surprisingly hard to answer them on the spot. Jot down quick notes on the things you know tend to come up:

  • Eating: general appetite, any new foods, feeding or picky-eating patterns
  • Sleep: bedtime, number of wake-ups, naps
  • Milestones: new words, movements, or skills you've noticed (or haven't)
  • Behavior and mood: anything new at home, daycare, or school
  • Screen time and activity: a rough daily estimate
  • Bathroom habits: for younger kids, any changes worth mentioning

You don't need a perfect log. A rough picture of a typical week is enough to help the conversation feel grounded in reality rather than guesswork.

Bring the Practical Records That Save Time

Write down (or gather) the administrative details that visits often stall on:

  • Current medications, vitamins, or supplements — names and how often
  • Any allergies or past reactions
  • Names of other specialists or therapists your child sees
  • Dates of recent visits, ER trips, or hospital stays
  • Any forms you need signed (school, sports, daycare, camp)
  • Questions about vaccines that are due or that you want to understand better

Having these on paper means you won't be the parent texting the other caregiver mid-appointment trying to remember a medication name.

Q&A: What's the Single Most Important Thing to Write Down?

If you only do one thing, write down your top concern in one clear sentence, and bring it up first. Visits tend to fill with routine items, and the thing that's been worrying you most is the easiest to run out of time for. Leading with it ensures it gets attention. ClearPath Pediatrics often reminds families that the opening minute of a visit is prime time — use it for what matters most to you.

Q&A: How Do I Keep Track of What the Provider Says During the Visit?

Leave space under each of your questions to write the answer, or ask if you can take notes or record the key instructions. Before you leave, jot down three things: what the provider said about your concerns, any next steps (referrals, follow-ups, labs), and when the next visit should be. If a referral or follow-up is mentioned, write down who's responsible for scheduling it so nothing slips through the cracks afterward.

A Quick Template You Can Reuse

Keep a repeatable format so each visit builds on the last:

  • Top 3 concerns (with dates/details)
  • Everyday notes (eating, sleep, milestones, behavior)
  • Records (meds, allergies, specialists, forms)
  • Answers and next steps (filled in during the visit)

Over time, these notes become a running story of your child's growth — useful for future visits, new providers, or anyone stepping in to help.
