How to Read a Pediatric Prior Authorization Denial Letter: A Parent's Guide

September 21, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric prior authorization denialinsurance denial letterpediatric appealsprior authorizationparent insurance guide

Opening a denial letter for your child's care is stressful. The language is formal, the layout is dense, and it can feel like the door has closed. Take a breath — a denial letter is not the end of the road. It is actually a document full of information you can use, once you know where to look. This guide walks you through the parts of a pediatric prior authorization denial letter so you can read it calmly and understand what it's telling you.

Remember: this is about helping you understand and organize the letter you received. It is not medical advice, and any decisions about your child's care belong to your licensed providers.

Start by Confirming the Basics

Before you read a single clinical detail, check the top of the letter for the facts that identify what was denied. Look for:

  • Your child's name and member ID — confirm the letter is actually about your child and the right plan.
  • The date of the letter — this often starts the clock on appeal deadlines.
  • The service, medication, equipment, or procedure that was requested.
  • The provider or facility who submitted the request.

Find the Reason for the Denial

Every denial letter must state why the request was turned down. This section is the most important part of the whole letter, and it usually appears under a heading like "Reason for Determination" or "Explanation." Denials generally fall into a few broad categories:

  • Medical necessity — the plan says the submitted information didn't show the service met their criteria.
  • Missing information — paperwork, notes, or documentation were incomplete.
  • Out-of-network — the provider or facility isn't in your plan's network.
  • Coding or administrative errors — a billing code, date, or detail didn't match.
  • Coverage or benefit limits — the item isn't covered under the plan, or a limit was reached.

The reason matters because it points to what may be needed next. A missing-information denial is very different from a benefit-exclusion denial, and knowing which one you're facing helps you and your provider's office respond appropriately.

Q&A: What Does "Not Medically Necessary" Mean on a Denial Letter?

It usually means the insurer reviewed the request against its own written criteria and decided the documentation submitted did not, in their view, demonstrate that the service met those criteria. Importantly, this is an insurance coverage determination — it is not your doctor changing their clinical opinion, and it does not mean your child doesn't need the care. It often means more detailed notes, records, or a peer-to-peer conversation between your provider and the plan may be part of the next step. Your child's licensed provider is the right person to interpret the clinical side of this.

Q&A: Where Do I Find the Appeal Deadline?

Look for a section titled "Your Appeal Rights," "How to Appeal," or "Reconsideration." It will list how many days you have to file, where to send the appeal, and what to include. This is time-sensitive — deadlines can be as short as a few weeks, so note the date immediately. The letter should also tell you whether an expedited (urgent) appeal is available and how to request one, along with a phone number for questions.

Note the Contact and Rights Section

Toward the end, the letter typically includes phone numbers for member services, information about requesting the specific criteria used, and your right to submit additional documentation. Some plans also mention external review options if the internal appeal doesn't resolve things. Keep the whole letter — every page, including the back — in your child's records.

Turn the Letter Into an Action List

Once you've read it, write a short summary for yourself: what was denied, the stated reason, the reference number, and the appeal deadline. Then share it with your child's provider's office, since they often handle the clinical documentation for appeals.
