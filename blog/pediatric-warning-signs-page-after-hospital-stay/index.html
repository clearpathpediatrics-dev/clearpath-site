How to Build a Pediatric Warning-Signs Page After a Hospital Stay

October 8, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric dischargewarning signs pagehospital discharge instructionspediatric care navigationcoming home from hospital

Coming home after a hospital stay is a relief — but it can also feel overwhelming. The discharge folder is thick, the instructions are long, and somewhere in there is the part that matters most to you: what to watch for and what to do if things change. A warning-signs page pulls that information into one clear, calm place your whole family can rely on. It doesn't replace anything your child's medical team told you. It simply organizes it so no one has to go digging through paperwork during a stressful moment.

Here's how to build one, step by step, using only the instructions your licensed providers have already given you.

Start With the Instructions You Already Have

Your warning-signs page should never contain anything you invented or guessed. Everything on it comes directly from your discharge paperwork, your care team's written notes, or what a provider told you out loud (which you then confirmed in writing). Gather these sources first:

  • The printed discharge summary and instructions
  • Any "call us if" or "return to the ER if" sections your team wrote
  • Specialist follow-up notes
  • Medication lists and schedules

As you read, highlight any sentence that describes a sign, symptom, or change your team asked you to watch for. Those highlighted lines become the backbone of your page. If something is unclear or you can't find a specific instruction, write down the question and ask your provider — don't fill the gap with your own interpretation.

Organize It Into Three Simple Zones

Parents find it easiest to sort warning signs the way many hospitals already do: by urgency. You can adapt this to match your team's exact wording, but a three-zone layout keeps everyone calm and clear:

  • Routine / expected: Things your team said are normal during recovery and generally handled at the next scheduled visit.
  • Call the office: Changes your team asked you to report by phone, with the exact number and hours.
  • Urgent / emergency: The signs your team listed for calling 911 or returning to the emergency department right away.

Use your providers' own words wherever possible. If your discharge sheet says "call if feeding drops below" a certain amount, copy that number exactly rather than rephrasing it. The goal is accuracy, not editing.

How do I know what counts as a warning sign for my child?

You don't decide this on your own — your child's licensed care team does. A warning sign belongs on your page only if a provider specifically named it in your discharge instructions or follow-up notes. If you're unsure whether something qualifies, that's a perfect question to ask before you leave the hospital or at your first follow-up call. ClearPath Pediatrics often helps families turn a verbal "watch for this" into a written, confirmed line they can trust. When in doubt, leave it off the page and ask your provider to clarify.

Add the "Who to Call" Details

A warning sign is only useful if the next step is obvious. Under each zone, write the exact contact information so no one is searching during a hard moment:

  • Your pediatrician's office number and after-hours line
  • The discharging hospital unit or nurse line, if provided
  • Any specialist your team told you to contact for specific issues
  • Your pharmacy
  • The clear note: for emergencies, call 911

Include your child's basics at the top — full name, date of birth, key diagnoses as written by the team, current medications, allergies, and your name and number. This makes the page useful for a grandparent, babysitter, or school nurse who may need it.

What should a pediatric warning-signs page actually look like?

Keep it to a single page if you can. Use large, readable text and short lines. Many parents post one copy on the refrigerator, keep one in the diaper bag or backpack, and save a photo on their phone. Review it with every caregiver who watches your child so everyone reacts the same way. Update it after each follow-up appointment, because instructions often change as your child recovers.

Keep It Current and Shared

Recovery is not static. At every follow-up, ask: "Has anything on my warning-signs list changed?" Cross out what no longer applies and add what's new, always based on provider guidance. Date the page each time you revise it so the whole family knows they're looking at the latest version.

Building this page is one of the most reassuring things you can do in those first days home.
