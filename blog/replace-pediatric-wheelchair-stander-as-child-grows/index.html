How to Get a Pediatric Wheelchair or Stander Replaced as Your Child Grows

September 15, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric wheelchair replacementpediatric standerdurable medical equipmentDME insurancegrowing child mobility equipment

If your child uses a wheelchair or stander, you already know these aren't things you buy once. Kids grow — sometimes in quick bursts — and equipment that fit perfectly last year can start to pinch, tilt, or stop supporting your child the way it should. Replacing pediatric durable medical equipment (DME) is a process with a lot of moving parts, but when you understand the steps, it becomes far less overwhelming. Here's how to stay ahead of it.

Start Before the Equipment Stops Fitting

The single most helpful thing you can do is begin early. Insurance approvals, evaluations, and manufacturing lead times can stretch over several months — and a child who has outgrown their chair or stander in the meantime is uncomfortable and less well supported.

Watch for signs your provider or therapist has flagged as reasons to reassess fit: knees crowding footplates, straps that no longer sit right, or your child sitting or standing at an angle that looks off. When you notice changes, jot them down with the date. Those notes become useful evidence later. As a general rhythm, many families revisit fit around growth spurts, at annual therapy check-ins, or whenever the equipment starts working against your child instead of for them.

Who's Involved and What Each Person Does

Replacing pediatric mobility equipment usually takes a small team, and knowing who does what saves you from chasing the wrong person:

  • Your child's physician or specialist writes the prescription and the letter explaining why new equipment is medically necessary.
  • A physical or occupational therapist often performs the seating or standing evaluation and measures your child.
  • The DME supplier (the equipment company) submits paperwork to insurance, orders the equipment, and delivers and fits it.
  • Your insurance plan or Medicaid program reviews the request and decides on coverage.

Keep the contact information for all four in one place. When something stalls, you'll want to call the right link in the chain quickly.

How Do I Actually Start the Replacement Process?

Begin by contacting your child's prescribing provider and current DME supplier and telling them the equipment no longer fits well. Ask your provider whether a new seating or standing evaluation is needed — for growing children, insurers often require an updated evaluation and measurements. From there, the provider writes the prescription and a letter of medical necessity, the therapist completes the evaluation, and the DME supplier bundles everything and submits it to your insurance. Your job is to keep the pieces moving and confirm each handoff actually happened rather than assuming it did.

What Documentation Will Insurance Usually Want?

While every plan differs, replacement requests for pediatric equipment commonly ask for: a current prescription, a recent seating or standing evaluation with measurements, a letter of medical necessity describing why the current equipment no longer works and why a specific replacement is needed, and sometimes documentation of how much the child has grown or changed since the last equipment was approved. Because "the child grew" is one of the clearest justifications, dated notes and growth-related measurements from your therapy visits are genuinely valuable. Ask your supplier for a checklist of what your particular plan requires so nothing gets bounced back.

Keep the Process From Stalling

Most delays come from missing paperwork or a step that quietly sat in someone's inbox. A few habits help:

  • After each appointment, ask, "What's the next step, and who does it?"
  • Confirm the DME supplier received the prescription and evaluation — don't assume.
  • Ask for a reference or authorization number once the request is submitted.
  • Save every document, letter, and denial in one folder so appeals are easier.
  • If you get a denial, ask specifically what was missing; many are fixed with added documentation.

None of this is about diagnosing anything or deciding what equipment is right — those calls belong to your child's licensed providers. Your role is organization, timing, and follow-through, which is exactly where families most often need a hand.
