What Pediatric Balance Billing Is — And When It's Not Allowed

November 12, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

If you've ever opened a bill after your child's care and seen a charge that seemed to come out of nowhere — a number your insurance didn't cover, from a provider you don't remember choosing — you may be looking at a balance bill. It's one of the most confusing parts of paying for pediatric care, and the rules around it have changed a lot in recent years. This guide walks you through what balance billing is, when it is not allowed, and how to organize your paperwork so you can ask the right questions.

What is balance billing?

Balance billing happens when a provider bills you for the difference between what they charged and what your insurance agreed to pay. For example, if a provider charges $1,000, your insurance allows $600 and pays its share, the provider might send you a bill for the remaining $400. That leftover amount is the "balance."

This most commonly happens with out-of-network providers, because in-network providers have agreed to accept your plan's negotiated rate as payment in full. It's important not to confuse balance billing with your normal cost-sharing — your copay, deductible, or coinsurance are amounts you're expected to owe, and those are different from a surprise balance bill.

When is balance billing not allowed?

A federal law called the No Surprises Act, which took effect in January 2022, protects families from many surprise balance bills. Under this law, you generally cannot be balance billed in situations where you couldn't reasonably choose an in-network provider, including:

  • Emergency care — if your child needs emergency services, you're protected from surprise out-of-network bills, even at an out-of-network facility.
  • Out-of-network providers at an in-network facility — for example, if your child has a procedure at an in-network hospital but an out-of-network anesthesiologist or lab provider was involved, you're usually protected.
  • Air ambulance services — out-of-network air ambulance transport is covered by these protections (ground ambulances are often handled differently by state law).

In these cases, you should only owe your normal in-network cost-sharing. Many states also have their own balance billing laws that add protections on top of the federal rules.

Can I ever be asked to waive these protections?

In some non-emergency situations, an out-of-network provider may ask you to sign a form agreeing to give up your No Surprises Act protections ahead of time. You are never required to sign this. The law says these protections cannot be waived for emergency care or for many ancillary services (like anesthesiology, radiology, and lab work) at an in-network facility. If you're ever handed a consent form you don't understand, it's completely reasonable to pause, take it home, and ask questions before signing.

How do I tell if a bill might be a surprise balance bill?

Start by comparing the bill against your Explanation of Benefits (EOB) from your insurer — the EOB is a statement, not a bill. Look for a line that says something like "patient responsibility" or "balance" that's larger than your expected copay, deductible, or coinsurance. Note whether the provider was in-network or out-of-network, and whether the care happened in an emergency or at an in-network facility. If the numbers don't match or the charge seems unexpected, that's a signal to call and ask. You can also contact your insurer and the provider's billing office, and the federal No Surprises Help Desk (1-800-985-3059) can explain your rights.

How to organize the paperwork before you call

Having your documents in order makes every conversation shorter and calmer. We suggest keeping:

  • The bill itself, with the date of service and provider name
  • The matching EOB from your insurance plan
  • Notes on whether the facility and each provider were in-network
  • A simple log of who you spoke with, the date, and what they said
