What a Pediatric Peer-to-Peer Review Is and How to Ask for One

November 10, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

Getting a denial letter for your child's medication, test, or procedure can feel like hitting a wall — especially when your doctor already said it's needed. One of the first steps many families don't know about is something called a peer-to-peer review. It's a built-in part of the appeals process, and understanding how it works can help you organize what your child's provider needs to move things forward.

This article walks you through what a peer-to-peer is, who does what, and the practical steps for asking for one. As always, the clinical decisions belong to your child's licensed providers — our goal here is to help you understand and navigate the process.

What Is a Pediatric Peer-to-Peer Review?

A peer-to-peer review (sometimes called a "P2P" or "doc-to-doc") is a phone conversation between your child's treating provider and a physician reviewer who works for the insurance company. It usually happens after a prior authorization request is denied. During the call, your child's doctor can explain the medical reasoning, answer the reviewer's questions, and share details that may not have come through in the original paperwork.

Think of it as a chance to add the human context behind the chart. Many denials are overturned simply because the treating provider got to explain the "why" directly to another physician. It is a provider-driven step — you, as the parent, generally cannot be on the call yourself, but you play a big role in making sure it happens and that your doctor is prepared.

How Do I Ask for a Peer-to-Peer Review?

Here's the practical sequence most families follow after a denial:

  • Read the denial letter carefully. It usually states the reason for denial and includes a deadline and a phone number for appeals. Peer-to-peer windows can be short — sometimes just a few business days — so timing matters.
  • Contact your child's ordering provider's office. Let them know the request was denied and ask whether they will request a peer-to-peer review. This is the key step, because the provider's office — not the parent — initiates the call with the insurer.
  • Offer to help gather supporting details. Ask the office what information would be useful: prior treatments tried, relevant specialist notes, or dates of earlier visits. Having these organized can make the provider's call stronger.
  • Confirm who will schedule it. Ask whether the office staff will call the insurer's peer-to-peer line, or whether you need to call first to request a date and time, then pass that to the office.
  • Write down names, dates, and reference numbers. Keep a simple log of every call: who you spoke with, when, and what they said.

What Happens After the Call?

After the peer-to-peer conversation, the insurer's reviewer makes a decision — the denial may be overturned (approved), upheld (still denied), or they may ask for more information. Your child's provider's office will usually be notified first, so check in with them if you don't hear anything within a few days.

If the denial is upheld, that isn't the end of the road. A peer-to-peer is only one layer. You still typically have the right to a formal written appeal, and sometimes an external or independent review. The denial letter will outline these next steps and their deadlines.

How Parents Can Stay Organized Through the Process

Denials and appeals move fast and involve a lot of moving pieces. A few habits make it easier:

  • Keep the original denial letter and any letters of medical necessity in one folder.
  • Track every deadline on a calendar — appeal windows are strict.
  • Ask the provider's office for a copy of any notes submitted on your child's behalf.
  • Stay in friendly, consistent contact with the office so the request doesn't stall.
