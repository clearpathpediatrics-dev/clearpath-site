How to Prepare a Pediatric Handover Document for a New Provider

September 19, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric handover documentcare team coordinationnew pediatric providerpediatric medical summaryparent care navigation

When your child sees a new provider — a specialist, a covering doctor, or a whole new team after a move — you often become the bridge between everything that came before and everything that comes next. A well-prepared handover document does that work for you. Instead of trying to remember dates and details under pressure, you hand over one clear packet that helps the new provider understand your child quickly.

This isn't about giving medical opinions or interpreting anything. It's about organizing what your existing providers have already documented so nothing important gets lost in the transition.

What a Pediatric Handover Document Actually Is

Think of it as a one-to-two page snapshot that answers the question a new provider is silently asking: "Who is this child, and what do I need to know before we start?" It pulls the highlights together in plain language so the provider isn't hunting through scattered records during a short appointment.

A strong handover document usually includes:

  • Basic identifiers: your child's full name, date of birth, and preferred name or pronouns.
  • Your child's diagnoses as your current providers have listed them — copied word-for-word, not reworded by you.
  • Current medications exactly as written on your most recent medication list, including doses and timing.
  • Allergies and reactions as documented by your providers.
  • Key contacts: current pediatrician, specialists, and their offices.

What to Include Beyond the Basics

The details that save the most appointment time are usually the practical ones. Consider adding:

  • A short timeline of major events — hospitalizations, surgeries, or significant changes — with approximate dates.
  • Equipment and supplies your child uses, such as feeding pumps, wheelchairs, or monitors.
  • Recent test results or reports your providers have shared, attached as copies.
  • Insurance information and any referrals or authorizations already in place.
  • Daily routines that matter clinically — how your child takes medication, communicates discomfort, or responds to new settings — described factually.

Keep the summary itself to one or two pages. Attach the supporting documents behind it rather than cramming everything onto the front page.

How Do I Prepare a Handover Document for a New Pediatric Provider?

Start with the records you already have. Pull your child's current medication list, allergy list, and most recent visit summaries from your patient portals. Copy diagnoses and doses exactly as they appear — never estimate or interpret. Then organize the information into a single top sheet with the basics and contacts, followed by attached copies of key reports. Save both a printed version and a digital one (PDF is easiest to email). Bring the printed copy to the first appointment and offer to send the digital version to the office ahead of time so it's in the chart before you arrive.

How Far in Advance Should I Send It?

Aim to send the digital version at least a few business days before the first appointment, and confirm the office received it. This gives the new provider time to review it rather than reading it for the first time while you sit in the room. Ask the front desk the best way to submit it — some offices prefer the portal, others a secure email or fax. Always bring a printed backup in case it didn't make it into the chart.

Keeping It Accurate Over Time

A handover document is only helpful if it reflects the present. After any appointment where a medication changes, a diagnosis is added, or a new specialist joins the team, update the master version the same week. Keep one "current" copy clearly dated so you never hand over an old one by mistake. If two parents or caregivers share the load, make sure you're both working from the same up-to-date file.
