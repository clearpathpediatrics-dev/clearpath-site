What to Write Down Before a Pediatric Well-Child Visit: A Parent's Prep Checklist

October 6, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric well-child visitwell-child checkup preppediatric appointment checklistparent visit notesdevelopmental check preparation

Well-child visits move fast. You have a handful of minutes with your provider, a wiggly child on the exam table, and a dozen things you meant to mention floating around in your head. By the time you buckle everyone back into the car, you remember the one question you really wanted to ask.

Writing a few things down ahead of time is the simplest way to fix that. You don't need anything fancy — a notes app or a scrap of paper works. The goal is to walk in organized so you leave feeling like you covered what mattered. Here's exactly what to jot down before you go.

Start With Your Questions and Observations

Your questions are the most important thing on the list, because they're the easiest to forget in the moment. Over the days leading up to the visit, keep a running note and add to it whenever something crosses your mind.

Helpful things to capture:

  • Your top 3 questions, in order of priority, so the most pressing ones get asked first.
  • Things you've noticed since the last visit — sleep patterns, eating, mood, energy, or anything that simply feels different to you.
  • Developmental milestones you've seen (new words, crawling, walking, climbing) or ones you've been wondering about.
  • Behavior or school notes, including anything a teacher or caregiver has mentioned.

Write these as plain observations — what you've seen and when — rather than guesses about what it means. That's your provider's role. Your job is to give them a clear picture.

Gather the Practical Details

Beyond your questions, a few factual items keep the visit running smoothly and help your provider have complete information in front of them.

Worth writing down:

  • Current medications and supplements, including the name and how often your child takes each one. (You don't need to recommend changes — just list what's being given.)
  • Allergies or reactions you've recorded.
  • Recent visits or tests with other providers, specialists, urgent care, or the ER, plus any results you were given.
  • Vaccine questions or records, especially if your child has received shots somewhere else.
  • Insurance or referral items — for example, if you're hoping to ask about a referral and want to understand the next steps.

Having these on one page means you're not digging through your phone or trying to recall dates while your provider waits.

How Far in Advance Should I Start My List?

A week before the visit is a comfortable starting point, but earlier is fine too. The real advantage comes from keeping the note open and adding to it as things come up day to day. Memories fade quickly, and the question that felt urgent on Tuesday is often gone by the appointment on Friday. A running list captures it while it's fresh. If you only have the morning of, that's still far better than walking in with nothing — even five minutes of jotting helps.

What Should I Write Down for a Developmental Check Specifically?

For visits that include a developmental check, note what your child is doing now across a few everyday areas: how they communicate, how they move, how they play and interact, and how they handle daily routines like eating and sleeping. Include specific examples when you can — "says about ten words" or "climbs stairs holding the rail" — because concrete details are more useful than general impressions. Also write down anything you've wondered about or that another caregiver has flagged. This gives your provider real-world information to work with during the check.

Keep It Simple and Bring It With You

You don't need a perfect document. A short, honest list beats a polished one you leave at home. Put it somewhere you won't forget it — your phone, the diaper bag, or taped to the car keys the night before.
