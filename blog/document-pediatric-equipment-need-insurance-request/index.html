How to Document Pediatric Equipment Need for an Insurance Request: A Parent's Guide

September 15, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric DME insurance requestdocumenting equipment needpediatric durable medical equipmentinsurance prior authorizationletter of medical necessity

When your child needs a piece of durable medical equipment (DME) — a wheelchair, feeding pump, oxygen concentrator, stander, or specialty seating — the insurance request often lives or dies on the paperwork. Insurers rarely say "no" to the need itself. More often, they say "we can't approve this with the information provided." The good news is that documentation is something you can organize, gather, and double-check ahead of time.

This guide walks you through what a well-documented pediatric equipment request usually includes and how to keep it all in one place. Your child's licensed providers make the clinical decisions and write the medical language — your job is to help make sure every piece is present, current, and easy to find.

What Documents Usually Go Into a Pediatric Equipment Request

Insurers typically want to see a consistent story told across several documents. While every plan differs, requests commonly include:

  • A prescription or order from your child's treating provider naming the specific equipment.
  • A letter of medical necessity (LMN) written by the provider explaining why this item — and this configuration — is needed.
  • Recent clinical notes that reference the same need (an evaluation, therapy note, or specialist visit).
  • An evaluation from the relevant therapist (physical, occupational, speech, or a seating clinic) when the equipment is specialized.
  • The supplier's quote or paperwork with product codes.

The most common reason for a stall isn't a missing item — it's inconsistency. If the prescription says one thing and the therapy note says another, expect follow-up questions. Ask your providers to keep the language aligned.

How Do I Document Why My Child Needs This Specific Equipment?

The strongest requests connect your child's day-to-day function to the item requested — and only your child's licensed providers can put that in clinical terms. What you can do is give them the raw material they need. Keep a short, dated log describing what happens without the equipment: how your child manages at home, at school, during meals, or with mobility, and where the current setup falls short. Note things like frequency, safety concerns you've observed, and how needs have changed as your child has grown.

Hand this log to the provider or therapist writing the letter. It helps them describe function specifically rather than generally, and specific descriptions tend to move requests forward. Avoid writing your own conclusions about what the equipment "treats" — leave the clinical reasoning to the provider and stick to what you see and experience.

Can Photos or a Home Video Help the Request?

Yes — when your provider or supplier asks for them. A photo of the current equipment that no longer fits, or a short clip showing a transfer or positioning challenge, can help a therapist document need accurately and can support a request or appeal. Only share images through secure channels your provider or plan specifies, and always ask first whether they're wanted. Never post identifiable photos of your child publicly. When you do share, label each file with the date and a one-line description so the reviewer isn't guessing.

Organizing the Paperwork Before You Submit

A little structure saves weeks. Try this simple approach:

  • Create one folder — paper or digital — labeled with the equipment and the date.
  • Put the prescription, LMN, evaluations, notes, and supplier quote inside, newest on top.
  • Write down the names, dates, and reference numbers of every call to the insurer.
  • Confirm whose office is actually submitting the request — the provider, the supplier, or you.

Before anything goes in, check three things: the dates are recent (many plans expect notes within a certain window), your child's name and plan ID are correct, and the equipment described matches across every document.

If the Request Is Denied

A denial is not the end. The denial letter should state a reason and an appeal deadline — read it carefully and note the date. Often the fix is straightforward: a missing signature, an outdated note, or a needed detail from the therapist. Gather what's requested, keep your organized folder handy, and work with your provider's office on the appeal.
