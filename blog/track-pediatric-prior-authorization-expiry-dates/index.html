How to Track Pediatric Prior Authorization Expiry Dates So Your Child's Coverage Never Lapses

August 27, 2026 · 4 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

When your child relies on a medication, therapy, piece of equipment, or ongoing specialist service, an insurance prior authorization usually has an expiration date. It's easy to assume approval means "done" — but most authorizations cover only a set window of time or a set number of visits. If that window closes before a renewal is submitted, coverage can quietly lapse, and you may not find out until a pharmacy, therapist, or supplier turns you away.

The good news: this is one of the most preventable problems in pediatric care. With a simple tracking habit, you can stay ahead of every expiry date and give your providers time to renew before anything runs out.

Start by Finding the Expiry Date on Each Authorization

  • The approval letter your insurance mailed or posted to your online member portal
  • The authorization number reference on a pharmacy or supplier receipt
  • Notes from your specialist's office that requested the authorization

If you can't find a date, call the number on the back of your insurance card and ask directly: "What is the expiration date and the authorization number for this service?" Write down what you're told, along with the name of the person you spoke with and the date of the call.

How Do I Actually Keep Track of Multiple Expiry Dates?

The simplest reliable method is a single running list — on paper, a spreadsheet, or your phone — that includes one row per authorization. For each one, capture: the service or item, the authorization number, the start date, the expiry date, which provider's office requested it, and a "renew by" reminder date.

Set that "renew by" date 30 to 60 days before expiry. Renewals often require fresh documentation from your child's provider, and that takes time. Add the reminder to your phone calendar with an alert, so the date finds you instead of you having to remember to look.

What's the Difference Between a Date-Based and a Visit-Based Authorization?

Some authorizations expire on a calendar date. Others expire once a certain number of visits or units has been used — for example, a set number of therapy sessions. Both can "run out," but you track them differently.

For date-based ones, watch the calendar. For visit-based ones, keep a simple tally of visits used versus visits approved, and treat "a few visits left" as your trigger to start a renewal. Many families track both in the same list, with a column noting whether the limit is a date, a count, or both. When in doubt, your insurer can confirm exactly how a specific authorization is measured.

Build a Renewal Routine That Runs on Autopilot

Tracking works best when it becomes a rhythm rather than a scramble. A few habits that help:

  • Do a monthly 10-minute review. Scan your list, check what's expiring in the next two months, and flag anything that needs action.
  • Loop in the right office early. The provider who ordered the service usually initiates the renewal, so give them your "renew by" heads-up in writing.
  • Confirm the renewal actually went through. Don't assume — ask for the new authorization number and new expiry date, then update your list.
  • Keep every approval letter in one folder, physical or digital, so you're never hunting during a stressful moment.

This is exactly the kind of behind-the-scenes coordination that quietly protects your child's care. At ClearPath Pediatrics, our RN care navigators help families organize authorization timelines, set renewal reminders, and understand which office handles what — so nothing falls through the cracks.

What Should I Do If an Authorization Already Expired?

First, don't panic. Contact the provider's office that ordered the service and let them know it lapsed, and ask them to begin a new authorization request right away. Ask your insurer whether there's a way to backdate or expedite given the gap. Keep notes of every call. While lapses are stressful, they're often fixable — the priority is getting a fresh request moving and understanding the timeline for your specific plan.

Remember that decisions about your child's medical care always belong to your licensed providers. Your job here is simply organization and timing — making sure the paperwork keeps pace with the care.
