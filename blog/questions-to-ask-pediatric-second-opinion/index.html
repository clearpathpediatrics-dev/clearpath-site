What Questions to Ask at a Pediatric Second Opinion: A Parent's Checklist

August 25, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric second opinionspecialist referral questionspediatric care navigationparent appointment prepsecond opinion checklist

Seeking a second opinion for your child isn't a sign you distrust your first provider — it's a normal, healthy part of making big decisions about your child's care. But a second opinion is only as useful as the questions you bring to it. When you walk in with a clear list, you turn a nerve-wracking appointment into a focused conversation. This guide gives you practical questions to ask, organized so you can leave with more clarity, not more confusion.

Remember: the goal isn't to decide who's "right." It's to understand your options well enough to make an informed choice with your child's licensed providers.

Questions About the Diagnosis and How It Was Reached

Start by understanding how this provider sees the full picture. You're not asking them to overrule anyone — you're asking them to walk you through their reasoning so you can compare perspectives.

  • Do you agree with the current understanding of my child's situation? If not, what do you see differently, and why?
  • What did you look at to reach that view — records, imaging, labs, an exam, or a combination?
  • Is there any additional information or testing you'd want before you feel confident?
  • How certain are you, and what would change your assessment?

Bring your child's records, test results, and medication list so this provider has the same information the first one did. That single step often makes a second opinion far more meaningful.

Questions About Treatment Options and Next Steps

This is where a second opinion often pays off most. Different providers may weigh options differently, and hearing more than one approach helps you understand the range of what's reasonable.

  • What options would you consider, and how do they compare?
  • What are the benefits, risks, and trade-offs of each approach as you see them?
  • What happens if we wait, monitor, or do nothing for now?
  • How will we know whether a plan is working, and when would we reassess?
  • Who would coordinate this care, and how does it fit with my child's other providers?

Take notes on the reasoning behind each answer, not just the recommendation itself. Understanding why a provider suggests something helps you have a more productive conversation with your original team afterward.

Is It Rude to Tell a Provider You're Getting a Second Opinion?

No — and most experienced pediatric specialists expect it. Second opinions are a routine, respected part of medicine, especially for complex conditions or major decisions. You can simply say, "We're gathering perspectives so we can feel confident about the path forward." A good provider will support that. Being open also helps the new provider know what to focus on and makes it easier to share records between offices, so nothing falls through the cracks.

What Should I Bring to a Pediatric Second Opinion Appointment?

Bring everything that helps the new provider see the same picture your first provider saw. That typically includes your child's relevant medical records, recent test and lab results, imaging (or instructions for accessing it), a current medication and dosage list from your child's prescribers, and a short written summary of your child's history. Also bring your top three questions written at the top of the page so you ask them even if the visit feels rushed. Organizing these ahead of time is exactly the kind of preparation the team at ClearPath Pediatrics helps families put together.

Questions to Ask Before You Leave the Room

The last few minutes matter. Before you go, make sure you understand the practical logistics of what comes next.

  • Can you send a written summary of your opinion to me and to my child's other providers?
  • What should I ask my original team based on what you've told me today?
  • If we move forward, what are the first steps, and who initiates them?
  • Who do I contact with follow-up questions, and how?

Write down the answers while they're fresh. If two providers disagree, that's not a failure — it's useful information you can bring back to the family conversation and to your child's licensed care team, who make the final clinical decisions.

A second opinion works best when you're organized, calm, and clear about what you want to learn.
