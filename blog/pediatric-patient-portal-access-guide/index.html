How to Get Pediatric Patient Portal Access to Every System Your Child Is Seen In

September 13, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric patient portalmedical records organizationpediatric care navigationchild health recordsparent portal access

When your child sees a pediatrician, two specialists, a hospital lab, and an imaging center, their medical information usually doesn't live in one tidy place. Each of those settings often runs its own patient portal, and each one may need to be set up separately. If you've ever felt like you're chasing test results across a dozen logins, you're not imagining it. The good news: getting organized access is very doable once you approach it system by system.

This guide walks you through how to track down, request, and manage portal access across every place your child is seen — so you spend less time hunting and more time actually using the information.

Start by Making a List of Every System Your Child Touches

Before you request anything, you need a full picture. Grab a notebook or open a simple document and write down every place your child has received care in the past year or two. Think broadly:

  • Your primary pediatrician's office
  • Every specialist (cardiology, neurology, GI, etc.)
  • Any hospital or urgent care visited
  • Standalone labs and imaging centers
  • Therapy providers (PT, OT, speech, behavioral)
  • Dental and vision if relevant

Next to each one, note whether you already have portal access, and if so, which portal it is. You'll quickly notice that some large health systems share a single portal (like MyChart) across many locations, while smaller practices use their own. This list becomes your master checklist.

How Do I Actually Request Portal Access for My Child?

For each provider on your list, the fastest route is usually to ask the front desk or medical records department directly. Say: "I'd like to set up parent portal access to my child's records. What's your process, and is there a form I need to sign?" Most offices will either email you an invitation link or hand you a signup code at your next visit.

Have this information ready when you ask: your child's full legal name, date of birth, and the email address you want tied to the account. Some systems ask for a photo ID or a signed authorization confirming you're the parent or legal guardian. If a portal invitation was sent long ago and expired, simply ask them to resend it — expired links are extremely common and easy to fix.

Understand Proxy Access and Why It Matters for Kids

Children's records work differently from adult records. Because a child can't legally manage their own account, parents typically get what's called proxy access — permission to view your child's portal under your own login. Setting this up correctly is what lets you see everything, rather than getting locked out.

A few things to know as you go:

  • You may need separate proxy setup for each child, even within the same system.
  • Some portals let you link multiple children to one parent login — ask if that's possible.
  • Access rules can change as a child gets older (many systems adjust teen access around adolescence), so it's worth confirming what you'll be able to see.

What If a Provider Says They Don't Have a Portal?

Smaller practices, some therapy providers, and certain labs may not offer an online portal at all. That doesn't mean you're stuck. You can request records the traditional way by asking the office for a copy of your child's records or asking them to send results directly to your pediatrician. Under federal rules, you generally have the right to obtain a copy of your child's medical records, though there may be a reasonable processing time. Keep a note in your master list marking these "no portal — request by email/fax" so you remember how to reach each one.

Keep It All Manageable Once You're In

Once you've collected logins, store them securely — a password manager is ideal, but even a locked note works better than sticky notes. Bookmark each portal in one folder in your browser. When new results come in, you can save PDFs into a single organized folder on your computer by date and provider. That way, when a specialist asks "what did the last labs show?", you can find it fast, and you're organizing information your licensed providers gave you — not interpreting it yourself.
