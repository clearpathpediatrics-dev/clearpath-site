How to Appeal a Denied Pediatric DME or Equipment Request: A Parent's Step-by-Step Guide

October 14, 2026 · 5 min read · ClearPath Pediatrics

pediatric DME appealdenied equipment requestpediatric prior authorizationinsurance appealdurable medical equipment

Opening a letter that says your child's wheelchair, feeding pump, orthotics, or other durable medical equipment (DME) has been denied can feel discouraging — especially when a provider told you it was necessary. Take a breath. A denial is rarely the final answer. It's the start of a process called an appeal, and many families succeed when they stay organized and move step by step. This guide walks you through how to prepare an appeal so nothing slips through the cracks.

Everything below is about organizing and navigating the paperwork. Clinical decisions always belong to your child's licensed healthcare providers — your job is to gather, track, and submit what they've already recommended.

Start by Reading the Denial Letter Closely

Your denial letter is the roadmap for everything that follows. Before you do anything else, read it carefully and highlight a few key details:

  • The exact reason for the denial (for example, "not medically necessary," "missing documentation," or "out of network").
  • The specific equipment or billing code that was denied.
  • The appeal deadline — this is critical and is often 30, 60, or 180 days from the date on the letter.
  • The address, fax, or portal where appeals must be submitted.
  • Whether it was the insurer or the DME supplier that triggered the denial.

Write the deadline on your calendar immediately and set a reminder well before it. Missing the window is one of the most common reasons appeals fail.

What Documents Do I Need to Appeal a Pediatric DME Denial?

To appeal a denied pediatric DME request, you'll generally want to gather: a copy of the denial letter, the original prescription or order for the equipment, a letter of medical necessity from your child's provider, relevant clinical notes or evaluations that support the request, and a written appeal request from you. Call the prescribing provider's office and ask whether they can supply an updated letter of medical necessity that directly addresses the reason listed in the denial. Keep copies of everything you send, and note the date and method of each submission.

Organizing these into one folder — physical or digital — makes the whole process far less overwhelming and helps you respond quickly if the insurer asks for more.

Write a Clear, Focused Appeal

Your appeal letter doesn't need to be long or sound legal. It needs to be clear and to point directly at the denial reason. A simple structure works well:

  • Your child's name, date of birth, member ID, and the claim or reference number.
  • A sentence stating you are requesting an appeal of the denial dated [date].
  • A sentence noting the equipment was prescribed by your child's provider.
  • A reference to the attached supporting documents.
  • Your contact information and the best way to reach you.

Let the provider's letter of medical necessity carry the clinical explanation — you don't need to argue the medicine yourself. Your role is to make sure the right paperwork reaches the right place on time.

How Many Levels of Appeal Are There?

Most plans offer more than one level. The first is usually an internal appeal, reviewed by the insurer itself. If that's denied, you may be able to request a second-level internal appeal, and after that an external review by an independent organization not connected to your insurer. In some situations, a provider can request an expedited (urgent) appeal if a delay could seriously affect your child. Your denial letter and your plan documents will describe which options apply to you and the deadlines for each. If you're unsure which level you're on, call the number on the back of your insurance card and ask them to explain where your case stands.

Track Every Step

Keep a simple running log: the date you called or sent something, who you spoke with, any reference number they gave you, and what they said the next step would be. This log is invaluable if a submission gets lost or the timeline stalls. Follow up by phone a week or two after submitting to confirm your appeal was received and is in review.

Appeals take persistence, and it's completely normal to feel worn down by the back-and-forth — especially while caring for a child who needs the equipment now. Staying organized is the single most powerful thing you can control.
